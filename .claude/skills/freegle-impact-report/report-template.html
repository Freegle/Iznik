Welcome

Every day in the UK, more than 35,000 reusable items are thrown away at council tips. Over six million people cannot afford essential furniture. And councils face acute financial pressure. Freegle sits at the meeting point of all three problems, and quietly solves a piece of each.

We are a free, volunteer-run network that helps people give and get things for free, locally. There is no shop, no van and no charge. A member offers something they no longer need; a neighbour who needs it replies; they arrange the handover themselves. Multiply that simple act across nearly five million members and around 500 local communities, and it becomes one of the largest reuse movements in the country.

In 2025 that movement kept 10,910 tonnes of usable stuff in use, avoided 5,564 tonnes of carbon, saved local authorities an estimated £1.3 million in disposal costs, and put £11.1 million of value back into people's hands, almost all of it for free.

This report sets out that impact, how we measure it, and why we believe Freegle is one of the most cost-effective pieces of social and environmental infrastructure in the UK.

To reduce waste and alleviate poverty by increasing reuse, creating more sustainable and resilient communities across the UK.

What makes Freegle different

There are many ways to move on unwanted stuff: the tip, the skip, the charity shop, the resale app. Freegle is none of these, and that is exactly the point.

Free at both ends, and local

The charity shop charges. Vinted and eBay take a cut. The council skip costs you money and a trip. Freegle is the only national reuse service that is completely free at both ends and matches people within their own neighbourhood. Nothing is sold, so 100% of the value stays with the giver, the receiver and the local community. That removes the biggest barrier to reuse for people on the lowest incomes.

It runs on almost nothing

Because the members do the work, Freegle delivers millions of pounds of value on a cash budget of around £100,000 a year. No other model in the reuse sector comes close on cost-effectiveness.

Infrastructure, not a project

Freegle has run continuously for 16 years, in every part of the UK. It is always on, needs no premises, and scales with demand at zero marginal cost. A partner is not buying a pilot; they are plugging into a network that already exists in their area.

The one-line version

"It's like online dating for stuff."

How Freegle works

  • A member offers something, or asks for something they want
  • Neighbours reply; the member chooses who to give it to
  • They arrange a doorstep handover themselves, for free
  • Local volunteers keep each community welcoming and safe

National reach. National Reuse Day 2024 carried Freegle's message to millions through ITV regional news in 8+ regions, BBC News, The Guardian, Radio 4, Radio 2 and Money Saving Expert. Freegle's work has been recognised by the Ashden Awards, the National Recycling Awards and the Green Apple Awards.

Reuse sits above recycling on the waste hierarchy, yet it gets a fraction of the attention. Freegle's job is to make the highest, hardest tier the easy, obvious, everyday choice, for free.

How we measure it, honestly

Freegle records the weight of items as members complete exchanges, then applies WRAP's Benefits of Reuse tool to convert weight into carbon and value. Carbon is counted at 0.51 tonnes CO2e per tonne reused, a conservative whole-life figure; a sofa or fridge saves several times more.

We deliberately count conservatively. The factor already assumes only about half of reused items prevent a genuinely new purchase (the rest would have been met another way). And members often forget to confirm a successful handover, so the real totals are higher than we claim. Weight is the single number everything else is built on, and we would rather under-state it than over-claim.

Carbon in money terms. Valued at the Government's central carbon price (~£260/tonne CO2e), the 5,564 tonnes avoided in 2025 represents around £1.4 million of climate value to society. We show this to illustrate the scale of the externality; we do not add it to the £11.1m benefit, with which it may partly overlap.

Local economy & jobs

Money in pockets, jobs in the economy

Reuse is not only the greenest way to handle stuff, it is the most economically productive. It saves households money directly and supports the most job-rich end of the resource economy.

A lifeline in the cost-of-living crisis

For a family that cannot afford to buy new, free is transformational. Over six million people in the UK lack essential furniture such as a bed, sofa or cooker. Getting a sofa through Freegle saves a household around £940 against buying new (WRAP). Nationally, WRAP estimates current reuse already saves UK households about £1 billion a year.

The most job-rich tier of the hierarchy

Per 1,000 tonnes of material, reuse supports far more employment than the alternatives:

WRAP & Green Alliance (2015). At the upper bound, reuse is up to 200x more job-intensive than landfill. Freegle's 10,910 tonnes in 2025 represents reuse activity equivalent to roughly 90 to 220 jobs in the local reuse and repair economy.

Built for the circular economy ReLondon is creating

If London meets its 2030 targets, ReLondon projects the capital's circular economy could grow from 231,000 to over 500,000 jobs and add £24.2 billion of GVA. Freegle is the grassroots engine for exactly that: it normalises reuse among millions of residents and feeds local repair and reuse organisations with a steady supply of goods.

Keeping value local

Every Freegle exchange happens between neighbours. The goods never leave the area and the money a household saves stays in the local economy. Freegle also feeds local furniture-reuse charities and repair schemes, which have waiting lists and which directly employ local people, with the goods they need.

Levelling up, by design. Left to the market, circular-economy jobs cluster in London and the South East. Freegle's distributed, community-led model spreads the benefit to every part of the UK, including the most deprived areas.

Stronger communities

Resilience, connection and cohesion

Freegle's deepest impact is the hardest to weigh on a scale. A gift between strangers is a small act of trust, and millions of them a year weave more connected, more resilient, more neighbourly places.

Giving and receiving on Freegle does something a transaction never can. Research on gifting platforms comparable to Freegle (Ballard & Bhatta, 2012, on Freecycle and Craigslist) found their members show significantly stronger community identification, solidarity and willingness to give than users of buy-and-sell sites. The more people receive, the more they give back.

Connection has measurable value. The Government's Green Book wellbeing methodology and the HACT Social Value Bank value outcomes like a stronger sense of belonging to your neighbourhood at several thousand pounds per person per year. Freegle touches these at a scale almost no other service reaches. (We quote these illustratively; measuring them directly is our next priority, page 14.)

Resilience in a crisis. A network of neighbours already used to helping each other is what communities lean on when times are hard. Freegle quietly builds that social infrastructure every day.

"People referred to the foodbank often don't know where to get basic household items. They are often in temporary accommodation, or have recently come out of prison, and have nothing." Pat Gabriel, Wandsworth Foodbank

Wandsworth Council funded Freegle through its Cost-of-Living Support Fund to reach the households hardest hit by rising prices. Rather than cold outreach, Freegle mapped and built relationships with organisations already trusted by those communities: foodbanks, estates, a university and refugee groups.

The project worked through all six Wandsworth foodbank sites, recruited Community Champions on local estates, and connected the University of Roehampton with furniture-reuse charities to rescue white goods that were being thrown away. During the project, Wandsworth Freegle reused 110 tonnes, saving 56 tonnes of CO2e and generating £78,086 of social and economic value, a 5.2× return on the £15,000 grant. Of the 110+ organisations mapped, 30+ are still actively promoting Freegle after the project ended.

What it left behind

  • 30+ organisations still promoting Freegle post-project
  • Two new platform features for partners: "Always Wanted" lists and Charity Partner status
  • A database of 100+ local organisations, shared with the council
  • New supply chains between the university, Richmond Furniture Scheme and a repair charity
  • Continuation funding secured for a third phase

Wandsworth Foodbank alone supports 2,417 households a year. 60% of referrals are driven by low income; three in four guests are behind on bills. For them, free household essentials are not a nicety, they are a need.

The £78,086 is the WRAP-valued benefit of all reuse in the Wandsworth community during the project period, at the rate current at the time (£711/tonne; ~£112,000 at 2025 prices). The council's avoided disposal cost on those 110 tonnes (~£13,000-18,000) is additional public-purse benefit. "Engaged" here means identified and contacted; 30+ went on to actively promote Freegle.

The £45 figure is grounded in real data. In 2025 Freegle's 252 volunteer moderators carried out 412,094 recorded moderation actions. Valuing each at one minute gives around 6,900 volunteer hours, worth roughly £110,000 of labour at the UK median wage. Added to Freegle's full annual running costs of about £133,000, that is a total resource base of around £243,000, so Freegle still returns about £45 of WRAP-valued reuse for every £1 of everything it uses. These ratios rest on Freegle's own value of reuse, not speculative wellbeing valuations; a full Social Return on Investment study including the community, health and avoided-public-service benefits in this report would be higher still, peer reuse charities publish SROI figures of £8 or more to every £1.

Put simply: there are very few places where a council or a funder can turn £1 into £45 to £110 of waste prevented, money saved and community built, across the whole of the UK, with infrastructure that already exists. Freegle is one of them.

Who we are, and how we are run

Freegle is a serious, accountable organisation behind a deceptively simple idea. Here is who we are, how we are governed, and how we are funded.

Structure & governance

  • Freegle Ltd is a Community Benefit Society, registered with the Financial Conduct Authority and recognised as charitable by HMRC.
  • Run by a volunteer board of directors, with a small paid team and around 450 volunteers (252 actively moderating in 2025).
  • Operating since 2009; national coverage across ~500 local communities.
  • Local communities are self-sustaining; a small central input keeps the platform and support running.

Resilient by design. Because Freegle is distributed across hundreds of independent volunteer communities, it has no single point of failure. The platform runs whether or not any one volunteer is available.

How we measure, and what we will improve

An impact report is only as good as the honesty of its methods. Here is exactly how the numbers in this report are built, and where we are making them stronger.

The method

  • Weight is recorded from confirmed exchanges and is the foundation of every other figure.
  • Carbon = weight × 0.51 t CO2e/t (WRAP Benefits of Reuse, 2011 basis; a conservative blended figure already assuming ~50% of items displace a new purchase).
  • Value of reuse = weight × £1,019/t, which is WRAP's 2011 figure of £711/t uprated by Freegle to 2025 prices using ONS CPI.
  • LA savings use the same tonnage × WRAP gate fees (£121–164/t).
  • All 2025 platform figures are summed from Freegle's own dashboard for 1 Jan–31 Dec 2025.

Why conservative: we count only confirmed outcomes, use WRAP's established factors rather than higher in-house estimates, and present the three monetary lenses (reuse value, carbon value, LA savings) separately, never summed.

What we will strengthen next

  • A beneficiary survey to measure directly what members save and how reuse affects their wellbeing and sense of community, turning the illustrative wellbeing values in this report into evidenced ones.
  • An independently-assured SROI applying deadweight, attribution and displacement adjustments.
  • A council-level dashboard giving every partner their own tonnes, carbon and savings.
  • Re-running the 2024 WRAP tool on Freegle's current item mix to refresh the per-tonne factors.

Wellbeing proxy values (belonging, loneliness, volunteering) are quoted to show the scale of Freegle's potential social value. They are not applied to headline totals, because doing so credibly requires the beneficiary survey above. We under-claim rather than over-claim.

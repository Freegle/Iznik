# One case store, two kinds of reporter, and an agent that asks before it guesses

Freegle does not lack somewhere to put tickets. What it lacks is the back-and-forth that turns a vague report into something fixable: what exactly happened, to whom, how widely, and how urgently. Neither the commercial support industry nor the open-source helpdesks sell that back-and-forth in a form Freegle can use. The commercial products are priced for companies with paid support staff, none of them treats a self-hosted Discourse forum as a real two-way channel, and even the largest (Zendesk) leaves the grouping of many reports into one underlying problem as a manual step ([Zendesk Help](https://support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/4408835103898-Working-with-problem-and-incident-tickets)). The open-source options remove the licence cost but not the integration work, and none can look up a Freegle member live. What does exist, much of it published in 2025 and 2026, is research and engineering practice that answers the specific questions Freegle has been stuck on: ask only when a draft is genuinely uncertain, put all the questions in one message, judge urgency against a written rubric with worked examples rather than a bare label, and treat duplicate detection as a visible suggestion.

Freegle already runs most of the machinery this needs, in two systems that do not talk to each other: the monitor FSM, which triages Discourse and Sentry and can open pull requests and ask clarifying questions, and the ModTools AI Support Helper, which can resolve a member and pull their account, logs and errors. The recommendation of this report is to build, not buy: one shared case store in the main Freegle database, fed by both systems and by email, with an intake agent that behaves differently for the two very different populations who report problems. About 70 volunteer moderators on Discourse report on behalf of members and know the product; members who email support are one-off, unverified, and often upset. At the measured volume, roughly 110 Discourse reports and 20 escalated support threads a month, the model cost is trivial and the real cost is developer time, so the plan is phased so that every phase is useful on its own. Humans are consulted where they add value, but nothing in the design waits indefinitely for one: every state has a timer and a safe default.

## Commercial helpdesks price out a small charity and treat Discourse as an afterthought

Des Traynor's Intercom is the natural starting point since the brief singles it out, and the survey confirms it should stay ruled out. Fin's agentic "Procedures" slot-fill information through natural-language instructions rather than rigid forms, at $0.99 per resolved outcome on top of seats priced $29 to $132 a month ([Fin Help](https://fin.ai/help/en/articles/13976256-how-to-write-instructions-in-fin-procedures)). One secondary source reports that Salesforce agreed to buy Intercom for about $3.6 billion, completing in September 2026, with Fin pricing unchanged so far ([Gleap](https://www.gleap.io/blog/intercom-fin-ai-pricing-2026)); this was not confirmed from a primary source and should be checked before it is repeated. Either way, Intercom has no published nonprofit rate, only an ad hoc sales conversation. Fin's marketing claims a 76% resolution rate, but that figure only holds after Intercom changed its methodology in 2026 to exclude "Fin Constrained" conversations, the ones where escalation rules limited what Fin could do, from the denominator; independent, small-scale tests found materially lower real-world rates, with one 60-day test across four small-business clients averaging 38% against a marketed 50%, and a competing vendor's own estimate of production rates clustering at 45 to 53% ([via getmacha.com aggregation](https://www.getmacha.com/blog/intercom-fin-ai-explained)). That gap between marketed and observed performance recurs across the whole market and matters directly for Freegle: a charity with a thinner knowledge base and a messier, more idiomatic report format than a typical SaaS company should expect to sit well below whatever ceiling a vendor advertises, not near it.

The rest of the commercial field splits into groups that fail Freegle on different grounds. **Zendesk** is the most capable and expensive: its Intelligent Triage assigns High, Medium or Low confidence to intent, sentiment and language from the subject line and first comment, but its problem-and-incident linking, the exact mechanism Freegle needs to stop re-solving the same bug under ten different report threads, is explicitly manual, since "AI agent tickets can't be linked to problem and incident tickets" ([Zendesk Help](https://support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/4408835103898-Working-with-problem-and-incident-tickets)); a 20-agent team handling 3,000 conversations a month would run close to $80,000 a year once Copilot and per-resolution AI charges are added. **Front**, **Gorgias** and **Plain** fail on more specific grounds: Front's AI features are officially English-only, a limitation rather than a disqualifier for a mostly English-speaking UK charity, and its per-seat pricing still does not fit a volunteer team; Gorgias is built around Shopify-specific actions with no analogue in Freegle's reuse-listing workflows; Plain gives its AI agent away free but reads as a poor cultural fit for a volunteer moderator team, being aimed at technical, API-first customers. **Decagon** is enterprise-only with roughly a $50,000-a-year platform floor before any per-conversation charges, putting it out of reach on cost alone. The one genuinely novel pricing idea in the survey belongs to **Sierra**, Bret Taylor's company: it charges only when its agent resolves an issue autonomously and nothing when it escalates to a human, explicitly to align its own incentives with the customer's rather than billing for seats or volume ([Cheeky Pint podcast transcript](https://cheekypint.substack.com/p/bret-taylor-of-sierra-on-ai-agents)), a principle worth borrowing even though Sierra itself is entirely custom-quoted, enterprise-only, and carries no self-serve tier or nonprofit programme.

Underneath the pricing, the same structural gap shows up in every vendor: none ingest a self-hosted Discourse forum as a first-class channel. Zendesk's official Discourse plugin comes closest, converting a forum topic into a ticket and syncing comments one way, but a Discourse Meta thread from around September 2026 shows the community still doesn't know whether replies posted from Zendesk sync back to the forum ([Discourse Meta](https://meta.discourse.org/t/regarding-zendesk-integration-and-two-way-synchronization-with-legacy-discourse/411853)). Intercom's own Discourse plugins go no further than identity verification and a chat widget; everyone else needs a Zapier, Make or n8n bridge built specifically for the purpose. Adopting any of these platforms would not remove Freegle's forum-integration work, it would relocate it into glue code between two systems never designed to talk to each other, at roughly the engineering cost of building the equivalent logic directly. Nonprofit pricing does little to change the calculus: **Help Scout** is the only vendor surveyed with a published, standard, no-application 10% discount, stackable with its annual-payment discount ([Help Scout](https://docs.helpscout.com/article/596-billing-and-plans-guide)); every other vendor's nonprofit story is either a competitive grant most applicants won't win (Zendesk's Tech for Good awards, capped around 110 partner organisations globally) or an undocumented, US-centric sales conversation whose real outcome for a UK charity is unverified. Finally, the wider trust cost of a wrong AI answer is not hypothetical: peer-reviewed research published in 2026 found hallucinated support responses measurably increase negative word of mouth and erode loyalty ([SAGE Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00472875261456334)), and a Berkeley review of multiple surveys found 53 to 77% of respondents report a frustrating chatbot experience ([UC Berkeley CMR](https://cmr.berkeley.edu/2026/04/chatbot-frustration-is-real-hidden-costs-and-best-practices/)). For a volunteer-run charity that depends on community goodwill more than a typical SaaS business does, that risk argues strongly against ever letting an AI autonomously close a report, or a safety concern, without a human confirming it first.

## Open-source helpdesks solve the price problem but not the integration one

Moving to open source removes licensing cost but not the integration problem, and introduces operational risks the commercial platforms mostly hide behind their own hosting. **Chatwoot** is the most fully-featured candidate: its core is MIT-licensed, its Captain AI assistant is credit-metered rather than free, and its Dashboard Apps feature, an embeddable iframe that can show external context via postMessage, is the closest thing found to a cheap way of surfacing a Freegle member's account details inside a conversation. Two open GitHub issues limit how well that would actually work today: there is no agent-identity pass-through into the iframe (GitHub #5898) and no way to place it as a sidebar rather than a separate tab (GitHub #13305). Chatwoot's own production history is a caution rather than an endorsement: one operator reported attachment storage silently filling a disk in a way invisible to normal database-size monitoring, and a single 125-second slow query taking the whole product down for that installation. **Zammad** is the second serious option, and its own community forum documents a long-running pain point: Elasticsearch is a mandatory dependency, and keeping it healthy through upgrades is a recurring drain. Zammad 7 (March 2026) added AI ticket summarisation across seven providers, including bring-your-own-key options for OpenAI, Claude, Azure, Mistral, Ollama or a custom endpoint, but the same forum reports that local Ollama models "mostly do not work" for this feature because of its strict JSON-output requirement, and Zammad's contact matching only indexes contacts already imported into Zammad rather than doing a live external lookup, exactly the limitation a hypothetical live lookup against Freegle's own member table would need to avoid.

Two smaller projects matter more to Freegle's specific shape than their market share suggests. **RT (Request Tracker)** and its **Znuny** fork are the only options in the whole survey, commercial or open source, offering true bring-your-own-model AI with zero markup on top of the model provider's own cost. Znuny shipped an official "Znuny-LLM" add-on on 8 July 2026 that auto-proposes a queue, type, service and SLA from the first message and extracts structured data into ticket fields using plain-language rules, with a guided setup wizard and toggleable features for phased rollout, making it the closest existing open-source analogue to the bespoke system this report recommends below. **Libredesk**, built by the team behind the Listmonk mailing-list tool, is AGPL-3.0, written in Go with a Vue 3 and Shadcn frontend, a stack that mirrors Freegle's own Go API and Vue frontend closely enough to be worth naming, and ships its AI assistant and a human-approval-gated Agent Copilot free with no paid tier; its own documentation is explicit that the project is still alpha-stage. Across every option surveyed, only Zammad 7 and RT/Znuny avoid a paid subscription layered on top of the AI provider's own charges; Chatwoot's Captain and GLPI's GLPI-AI both require an ongoing paid tier in addition to model costs.

None of this changes the Discourse-integration verdict from the commercial side. Discourse's own discourse-ai and discourse-automation plugins can already triage and tag forum topics using an AI agent (recently renamed from Persona to Agent), and a new visual workflow builder announced 18 September 2026 extends that, but it is gated to Discourse's Business and Enterprise plans, and the one third-party plugin purpose-built to turn Discourse into a ticketing system, Pavilion's discourse-tickets, looks stale, with only 24 stars and 13 forks, no recent issue activity, and unclear compatibility with current Discourse. The considered verdict from the research is that Discourse is well suited to being an intake and triage layer feeding a separate system of record, not to being that system of record itself. A genuinely lightweight alternative worth naming is treating GitHub Issues, or Linear, as that system of record with an AI intake layer in front: GitHub's own Copilot-based accessibility-feedback triage already auto-fills around 80% of structured issue metadata, and Linear's Triage Intelligence, in public beta since 24 March 2026, resolves roughly 30% of Linear's own incoming bug reports by flagging duplicates against workspace history, though Linear is explicit this is not designed for high-volume frontline support and needs a dedicated inbox or chat channel in front of it. The honest conclusion for Freegle's member-database integration specifically is that no candidate offers a true live, read-only lookup against an external database out of the box; the cheapest realistic version of that feature, a Chatwoot Dashboard App calling a small internal read-only API Freegle would write itself, still requires Freegle to build the integration, just with less licensing cost spent on the shell around it.

## When to ask, when to stop, and why urgency needs a written rubric

The academic literature on bug reports converges on an uncomfortable finding: the fields developers say they need most, steps to reproduce, a working test case, a stack trace, are exactly the fields ordinary reporters find hardest to supply. A survey of 466 developers and users across Apache, Eclipse and Mozilla found this mismatch directly ([Bettenburg et al., FSE 2008](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1453101.1453146)), and a later manual analysis of roughly 3,000 real bug reports found that while 93.5% state what was observed, only 35.2% state what the user expected instead, and only 51.4% state clear reproduction steps, expressed across 154 distinct discourse patterns that make simple keyword or regex extraction unreliable ([Chaparro et al., FSE 2017](https://doi.org/10.1145/3106237.3106285)). Two systems built to address this are worth naming as design patterns rather than products to copy wholesale: Euler classifies each reported step as correct, ambiguous or vocabulary-mismatched and infers missing steps between the ones given, offering feedback rather than outright rejection ([Chaparro et al., ESEC/FSE 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07107)), and BURT is a guided chatbot that walks Android end-users through observed behaviour, expected behaviour and reproduction steps using a parser tuned against the app's own UI model, offering tap-through alternatives to typing wherever possible ([Song et al., ESEC/FSE 2022 / ICSE 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06050)). Neither answers the harder question: when should the system stop asking and just work with what it has?

The clearest answer to that question, and arguably the single most transferable finding in the whole research pass, comes from ClarifyGPT: rather than asking a fixed checklist of questions, it samples several candidate solutions to a task, executes them, and only asks a clarifying question when the candidates genuinely disagree with each other, which raised GPT-4's accuracy on a coding benchmark from 70.96% to 80.80% ([Mu et al., FSE 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10996)). Applied to Freegle's setting, this reframes the clarification problem from "what fields does this category require" into "draft the report from what's already known and only ask about the fields the draft is genuinely uncertain or contradictory about," which sidesteps the much harder, still-unsolved problem of parsing free text against a rigid schema. A separate, smaller body of work explains why systems tend to under-ask by default: the human preference data typically used to train these models is single-turn, so annotators never see what a clarifying question would have led to, and the model is never rewarded for asking one, a gap that training on double-turn preference data measurably closes ([ICLR 2025, arXiv:2409.00557](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00557)). Evidence on exactly how many questions to batch per turn is thinner and mostly vendor-anecdotal rather than controlled research; the one recurring, plausible principle is to batch every known-missing question into a single message per round on asynchronous channels like email or a forum, since a round trip there costs hours or days rather than seconds, and a good handoff to a human should always carry the full transcript, the AI's own confidence, and what it already tried, since poorly-handed-off cases take measurably longer to resolve once they reach a person ([Lorikeet 2026](https://www.lorikeetcx.ai/articles/ai-support-resolution-rate-what-the-numbers-hide-2026)).

Urgency and severity classification turn out to have the strongest single piece of quantitative evidence in the whole survey, and it is a warning rather than a reassurance. A study grading 171 real clinical-note cases found that two generations of the same model family, grading the same cases without a written rubric, agreed with each other at a Cohen's kappa of 0.177, statistically close to chance; after the researchers wrote a rubric with three explicit grade definitions, worked examples for each, and a four-step decision procedure, agreement between two entirely different model families rose to a kappa of 0.662, roughly four times higher, though the rubric-anchored graders also became systematically more severe than the unaided ones ([arXiv:2608.31016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.31016)). The direct implication for Freegle is that any point in the design where an AI assigns a category, a severity or a duplicate judgement needs a written rubric with worked examples and an explicit decision procedure in the prompt, not just a category name; a bare label like "urgent" or "safeguarding" invites exactly the kind of near-chance disagreement the study measured. The same principle shows up independently in security-adjacent literature recommending a CVSS or OWASP-style likelihood-times-impact grid, with likelihood banded as rare, occasional or frequent and impact banded as annoying, serious or catastrophic ([The Neural Base](https://theneuralbase.com/red-teaming-llms/learn/advanced/severity-classification/)), and it matches the ITIL framing on the operations side: priority is impact multiplied by urgency, conventionally on a 3-by-3 grid collapsing into five tiers, with impact and urgency weighted equally rather than urgency dominating (per PagerDuty's own published incident-priority guidance).

Deduplication research shows the same shape as the clarification research: nobody has fully automated the judgement, and the systems that work best treat AI as a suggestion engine rather than a decision-maker. Linear's Triage Intelligence combines embedding similarity, search and LLM reasoning to surface duplicate and related-issue suggestions with a visible explanation attached, deliberately trading one to four minutes of processing time per issue for that transparency, because trust in the suggestion was an explicit design goal, not just accuracy ([Linear engineering](https://linear.app/now/how-we-built-triage-intelligence)). A separate, smaller self-hosted tool called IssueSense uses plain vector similarity but deliberately never auto-closes anything, leaving the final judgement to a human, and a hobbyist project tuning its own duplicate-detection threshold found that a naive 0.9 cosine-similarity cutoff was too strict, since genuinely duplicate issues scored as low as 0.81 to 0.87 while unrelated issues scored around 0.49, meaning any such threshold needs tuning against labelled data rather than picked by intuition. ITIL's own vocabulary maps directly onto this: each individual report is an incident, in ITIL's sense of an unplanned disruption needing a fast, individual response regardless of root cause, while a cluster of incidents sharing an underlying cause is a problem, in ITIL's sense of something needing root-cause analysis, which may sit in a known-error state with a documented workaround while a permanent fix is pursued. That maps onto a data-model choice Freegle already half-uses for Sentry, where many error events roll up into one issue; the same one-to-many shape, one problem linked to many individual reports, is the natural structure for Freegle's own report-versus-problem split, addressed directly in the design section below.

## Safety, identity and the operating models that already work for volunteer organisations

Security research treats an AI support agent as a specific, well-understood attack shape, and Freegle's own setup, a public inbox anyone can email into, sits close to the worst case that literature describes. OWASP has ranked prompt injection as the top risk facing LLM applications in both its 2025 and 2026 editions, and its own worked example is a support chatbot: an attacker embeds an instruction in a message telling the model to ignore its guidelines, query private data or send emails on the attacker's behalf, leading to unauthorised access or privilege escalation, citing a real CVE issued against an LLM email assistant ([OWASP LLM01](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm01-prompt-injection/)). Simon Willison's widely cited "lethal trifecta" names the exact combination that makes this dangerous: private data, untrusted content, and the ability to communicate externally, all present in the same system at once, precisely the shape of an email or forum support agent whose tools can read member records and send replies. A dedicated study of email-specific injection attacks against real-world LLM email agents found a 73.54% overall attack success rate, and responsible disclosure from that research led to sixteen assigned CVEs across twenty-two vendors; the same study found generic "ignore previous instructions" attacks now reliably fail against premium models, meaning the attacks that do succeed are tailored rather than generic ([arXiv:2507.02699](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02699v2)). The defensive guidance that follows is specific and actionable: treat every word of inbound email or forum content as untrusted data, never as an instruction to the model; restrict what the agent's tools can actually do so that even a fully compromised model cannot reach an unintended recipient or action, because containment should not depend on the model making the right decision; and require human approval before any high-risk action such as sending a message at scale or altering another member's record ([Nylas guidance](https://cli.nylas.com/guides/email-prompt-injection-defense)). One defence recommended for internal agents, allowlisting the senders an agent will act on, is simply unavailable to Freegle, since members and prospective members email and post from addresses and accounts nobody controls in advance; that shifts essentially all the protective load onto tight tool permissions and a mandatory approval gate for the inbound path, rather than onto filtering who gets to talk to the system at all.

Two further risks sit alongside prompt injection and matter specifically because Freegle's reports are often about other people, not just about the reporter. GDPR treats a request phrased any way, on any channel, as a valid subject access request the moment it asks what information an organisation holds about someone, with no special form or keyword required, a one-month clock starting from the moment of receipt, and the right extending to anyone in the organisation who happens to receive it, meaning everyone must know what to do with one rather than assuming it will land with a designated compliance contact. Erasure, under Article 17, is a legally distinct right from access and must not be handled as if it were the same request. A change to UK law taking effect in 2026, the Data Use and Access Act, allows the response clock to pause from 5 February 2026 while reasonable clarification is sought from the requester, and requires organisations from 19 June 2026 to actively facilitate a complaint to the Information Commissioner's Office rather than merely tolerate one. Separately, because a Freegle report is frequently about another member (a complaint that someone isn't collecting an item they promised, say) rather than about the reporter's own account, any tool that can surface a third party's personal data risks leaking it straight back to the person who asked, a distinct GDPR-flavoured risk from the subject-access-request case and one the design needs to block explicitly rather than assume identity verification already covers.

Trust-and-safety research converges on treating human oversight as a structural property of the system rather than a courtesy: every escalation flag should be tied to a named decision owner, evidence, an available verdict, the paused state, and the resuming event, without those a flag is only a notification (arXiv:2608.13867, a finding from software-agent-oversight research whose principle transfers cleanly to a support-triage context), and automated actions should be reversible and audited by design. This lines up with older, non-technical evidence about how volunteer support operations actually run. Wikimedia's Volunteer Response Team, formerly OTRS, has operated a trusted-volunteer model since September 2004, requiring a strong track record and a signed confidentiality agreement before an agent can join, and roughly six administrators manage over four hundred agents partly by using queue-routing on the incoming email address as a coarse first triage step; the team migrated its underlying software from OTRS to Znuny in April 2021 but deliberately kept the team's own name unchanged through that migration, on the reasoning that renaming the team every time the tool changes causes needless disruption. Mozilla's SUMO support system goes further still: Mozilla runs no official company support channel for it at all, describing itself as "scaled by the community," with an established path for a community-handled report to become an engineering issue when it needs to. A 2018 time-boxed volunteer sprint specifically to answer Play Store reviews reflects a principle that generalises directly to Freegle's multi-channel reality: users report wherever is convenient for them, not wherever the organisation would prefer, so a design that only watches its preferred channel will always be missing some of its own bug reports. A 2017 governance lesson from the same community is a caution rather than a template: informal volunteer "leader" hierarchies caused inconsistency and power accumulation until the roles were made explicit as "Community Coordinators," a reminder that Freegle's own moderator-to-developer escalation path should name roles and authority explicitly rather than let them accrete informally. Underneath all of this sits a delivery-model choice: swarming, where whoever opens a case keeps it and pulls in help as needed, is repeatedly recommended over a rigid tiered queue for teams that are small, handle complex or varied cases, and run at low-to-moderate volume, a closer match to Freegle's tiny paid dev team plus many volunteer moderators than the escalate-up-the-tiers model that might look like the obvious thing to copy from a call-centre-scale operation.

## Freegle already built half of this, twice, and neither half talks to the other

Freegle is not starting from nothing. It has two working systems that between them cover most of the ground this report surveys, but they cover different ground, serve different audiences, and share no records.

The **monitor FSM** is an unattended loop, built on the in-house `ai-flower` workflow engine, that watches Discourse and Sentry, keeps its state in a local SQLite file (`monitor-fsm/monitor.db`), opens pull requests, and posts to Discourse. Its main table, `discourse_bug`, held 663 reports at the time of writing, first seen between April and September 2026: 33, 136, 177, 60, 89 and 168 a month, so roughly 110 a month once it was running in earnest. Those 663 reports came from only 69 distinct reporters. Discourse is the volunteers' forum, so these are moderators reporting on behalf of their members, not members themselves, and that shapes the design below more than any other single fact. The outcomes were 316 fixed, 121 off-topic, 93 feature requests, 44 deferred, and the rest spread across open, question, needs-detail, duplicate, investigating and fix-queued. One column, `state`, carries both what kind of report it is (bug, feature request, off-topic) and where it is in its life (open, fixed, deferred), so any question about, for example, "open feature requests" cannot be answered from the table. A `sentry_issue` table exists but is empty.

The FSM already has the two outbound behaviours this design builds on. It may post, without approval, a question asking a reporter what a report is missing (which member, which group, which post, a screenshot), once per reporting post and recorded so the same person is not asked again. It may also post a fixed "possible fix applied, please retest" message once a fix is live. Anything that asserts how Freegle behaves, such as an answer to a moderator's question, is queued as a draft (`discourse_draft`, with `approved_at`, `posted_at` and `rejected_at`) and waits for a human on the dashboard. That split, where asking for facts is autonomous and asserting facts needs a human, is sound and the design keeps it.

The **ModTools AI Support Helper** (`ModSupportAIAssistant.vue` backed by `claude-agent-sdk/`) is a chat tool for support volunteers investigating one member. It must resolve the member first (`identify_user`), then pulls a per-member snapshot of around 69 tables, plus Loki logs and Sentry issues over a 90-day window, and answers from that. This is exactly the "use what is already known instead of asking" capability that the clarification research recommends, and today the FSM cannot use it. The Helper's only escalation is "refer to geeks", which emails a transcript with an `SR-XXXXX` reference; nothing is written to the database, so no later search, duplicate check or metric can find it. The member-facing `SupportLink.vue` is a `mailto:` link that adds the member's user id as a line of text, so member support requests arrive as unstructured email.

Three internal plans already reached several of this report's conclusions. The July 2026 Helper rebuild removed an anonymisation layer that stripped personal data before it reached the model, in favour of direct read-only access. A second July 2026 plan proposed replacing the Helper's single free-form prompt with a constrained flow of about twelve investigation paths and a three-way router (member investigation, bug triage, product question). It was prompted by a real incident where the Helper blamed a member's review flag on a community that had cleared it months earlier, and it was checked against 364 real reports and a year of the support mailbox. It found that member investigations, the only thing the Helper is designed for, were a minority; bug and regression reports were the largest group, and product or policy questions a further share. That router was never built: the Helper's code has no bug-triage path today, and its documentation still describes one large prompt. A third plan, to feed Sentry replay and device data into triage, is also unbuilt.

The FSM's own reliability record argues for written rules over prompt guidance. A review on 31 May 2026 (`monitor-fsm/SWEEP-FINDINGS-2026-05-31.md`) found at least nine real bugs the FSM had wrongly marked fixed, dismissed or deferred, one of them deferred even though an admin had acknowledged the bug in the same thread, plus a verdict recorded against a post that did not exist. It produced nine rules, such as "never mark fixed while a reporter says it is still broken" and "require a linked PR or reporter confirmation before marking fixed". Only two were shipped as code; the other seven remain guidance in the prompt. The FSM also fails silently: when its API key is out of credit, when the first model call returns only "prompt is too long", when its state file has been truncated to zero bytes, and when Discourse returns a 429 with the wait time in the response body rather than a `Retry-After` header (`.claude/rules/monitor-fsm.md`). In each case the loop looks alive and does nothing.

## The design

### Build, on the main database, from the two systems already running

Every commercial product surveyed either costs more than a volunteer charity can justify or leaves Discourse as one-way glue that Freegle would write anyway. The open-source helpdesks remove the licence cost but add a second system to host, upgrade and keep in sync with the member database, and none can look a member up live. Znuny's LLM add-on is the closest existing shape to what is proposed here (bring your own model, propose rather than decide, each feature switched on separately), and is worth reading as a reference, not installing. The recommendation is to build. This is an inference from matching the vendor gaps against Freegle's own architecture, not a measured comparison.

The part of the earlier draft of this report I disagree with most is where the records live. `monitor.db` is a SQLite file on the machine that runs the FSM. It cannot be seen from ModTools, it is not backed up with the rest of Freegle's data, and it disappears with the machine. A support case is Freegle data about Freegle members, and it belongs in the main database, created by Laravel migrations like every other table, with an `id` on every table. The FSM keeps `monitor.db` for its own working state (cursors, iterations, reviewer feedback) and reads and writes cases through an API, as the Helper already does. ModTools gets a Cases view for support volunteers. That is what makes the two halves one system: the Helper can open or add to a case, the FSM can use the Helper's member lookup, and both see the same history.

### Two kinds of reporter

The design treats reporters differently because they are different.

**Volunteers on Discourse** (about 70 people, reporting about 110 times a month) know the product, report repeatedly, and are usually reporting on behalf of a member. Their identity is known from their Discourse account, which maps to their Freegle account. What they most often leave out is which member, which group, which post, and when. The agent can ask them directly and briefly, and the right tone is colleague to colleague. They are semi-trusted: their account is known, but the text of their post is still treated as data, never as instructions.

**Members by email or in the app** (about 20 escalated threads a month in 2026, from a playbook built on 115 escalated support threads between January and June) are one-off, often upset, and not verified by the channel. From the app, the member is known because they are logged in. From email, the sender address is a claim, not proof, so nothing from their account is disclosed until the address is matched to the account and, for anything sensitive, confirmed by a link sent to that address. The agent may investigate their account to understand the problem, but it tells them only what they could see themselves.

Every case records the **reporter** and the **subject** separately. When a moderator reports for a member, or a member complains about another member, collapsing the two into one "user" is how one person's data ends up in a reply meant for someone else.

### Core records

| Record | What it is | Key fields |
|---|---|---|
| `support_people` | One row per person, whichever channel they arrive from | Freegle user id (nullable), verified flag |
| `support_identities` | A channel handle belonging to a person | channel (discourse, email, app, sentry), handle, first seen |
| `support_cases` | One reported thing | kind (bug, feature, question, service request, safety, data rights), status (new, awaiting reporter, awaiting human, in progress, fixed, closed), urgency tier plus the reasons for it, reporter id, subject id, problem id |
| `support_messages` | Every message in and out, on any channel | case id, channel, direction, text, written by AI or human, approved by whom |
| `support_problems` | An underlying cause shared by one or more cases | title, status (open, known issue with workaround, fixed), Sentry issue ids, PR links, fixed in |
| `support_facts` | What the agent looked up rather than asked | case id, field, value, source (account, Loki, Sentry, reporter) |

Kind and status are separate columns from the start, which is the FSM's `state` split done once. `support_facts` makes the "look it up, don't ask" behaviour auditable: a human reading a case can see which details came from logs and which the reporter said. The existing `discourse_bug` rows migrate in as cases, so history and the evaluation set carry over.

### Channels

Each channel adapter turns what it sees into messages on a case and resolves the person before anything else happens.

**Discourse** is the FSM's existing watcher, writing to the case store instead of only to `monitor.db`. **Email** to support gets a case the moment it arrives. Threading uses a reply token in the outbound reply-to address, with `Message-ID` and `In-Reply-To` headers as a fallback, because tokens survive forwarding and mail clients that rewrite headers. **In app**: `SupportLink.vue` becomes a short form that opens a case with the user id attached, which removes the identity step entirely for the channel where it is hardest by email. **Sentry and Loki** do not open cases on their own. They attach evidence to problems and raise urgency, because an error nobody has reported yet is a monitoring alert, not a support case. **The Support Helper**: "refer to geeks" opens or updates a case with the transcript, instead of sending an email that nothing can find.

### The clarification loop

The rule the research supports best is to draft first and ask second. The agent writes a complete draft of the case from everything already known: the message, the reporter's account, the subject's account if one can be identified, Loki activity around the reported time, and Sentry issues for that user or page. It records each looked-up detail in `support_facts`. It then asks only about fields that are still missing, or where the evidence conflicts. That is ClarifyGPT's "ask only when candidates disagree" rule applied to support ([Mu et al., FSE 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10996)).

What "complete" means depends on the kind of case:

| Kind | Needed before a human or the fixer acts | Usually found without asking | Asked only if still missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bug | who it affects, where (page, post, chat), what happened, what should have happened, when | account, device and app version, Loki trail, Sentry errors | what they expected; which member or post, if the reporter is a volunteer |
| "Still broken after the fix" | which fix, and whether they are on the fixed version | deployed version, app version from logs | a retest on the current version |
| Member problem (for example, a missing reply or a post not appearing) | which member, which post or chat | account, message and chat history | the member's email or post link |
| Product or policy question | what they are trying to do | group settings | one question at most |
| Feature request | the need behind the request | similar past requests | nothing; log it and link it |
| Safety or data rights | nothing; the AI does not gather information | none | none; goes straight to a named human |

Etiquette differs by audience but the rules are the same. All questions go in one message. Each question offers concrete choices where possible ("Is this in the app or on the website?"), and the message says in one line why it is asking. Volunteers get one short paragraph. Members get a plain, warm message that states what has already been checked, so they are not asked for anything the system already knows.

The stopping rule reaches an outcome whether or not anyone answers:

| State | Timer | What happens with no reply |
|---|---|---|
| Awaiting reporter, first ask | 2 days for members, 3 for volunteers | one reminder that restates only the missing items |
| Awaiting reporter, after reminder | 5 more days | the case proceeds on the best draft, marked "unconfirmed" in the fields still missing, and goes to fixing or routing anyway |
| Awaiting human approval of a draft | 1 working day | re-flagged; after a second day an auto-generated holding reply ("we have this, here is the reference") goes out, never a substantive answer |
| Fixed, awaiting reporter confirmation | 7 days | closed as fixed; any later reply reopens the same case |

At most two rounds of questions from the agent: one ask and one follow-up on anything the first answer made newly uncertain. After that the agent works with what it has. A case never sits waiting for a reporter or a human forever. Waiting on a person pauses the case for a set time, and when the time runs out the case moves on to a defined next step.

### Urgency as a written rubric

Unaided, two versions of the same model grading severity agreed with each other barely better than chance (kappa 0.177); with a rubric of defined grades, worked examples and a step-by-step procedure, two different models reached kappa 0.662 ([arXiv:2608.31016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.31016)). So urgency is decided by a written rubric kept in the repository, with Freegle examples for each tier, and every case stores the reasons as well as the tier.

| Tier | Test | Example | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Now | Safety or safeguarding, personal data exposed to the wrong person, or a core flow (post, reply, chat, sign in) down for everyone | a member's address shown to a stranger; nobody can post | a human is paged; the AI does not reply beyond acknowledging |
| 2 Today | A core flow broken for a group of members, or rising fast | several volunteers in different groups report failed photo uploads within a day, matching a Sentry spike | same-day fix attempt; volunteers told it is known |
| 3 Soon | One member blocked from giving or getting, with no workaround | one member cannot reply to any chat | next fixing run |
| 4 Queue | A workaround exists, or it is cosmetic | a layout glitch on one page | batched |
| 5 Route | Not a fault | feature requests, how-to questions | logged, linked, answered |

The tier is not fixed when the case opens. It is recalculated whenever the linked problem changes: a second unconnected reporter, reporters in more than one group, or a step up in the linked Sentry or Loki error rate each move the problem up one tier, and every case on the problem moves with it. A "still broken after the fix" report is never below tier 3, because it means a verdict was wrong. The rubric is exactly the part that is enforced in code, which is the lesson of the May sweep: rules that matter do not live only in a prompt.

### Grouping cases into problems

Each new case is compared with open problems by embedding similarity on its drafted summary. Above a high threshold it is linked automatically, and the link is shown on both the case and the problem so a human can undo it. Below a low threshold it is treated as new. In between, the model compares the two drafts against a short written rubric and links or declines with its reasons stored. The thresholds are tuned against the historical reports, not picked by feel; one published tuning exercise found genuine duplicates scoring as low as 0.81 where a guessed 0.9 cut-off would have missed them. Automatic linking is acceptable here, where Linear and IssueSense only suggest, because in this design a link is cheap to undo and is what raises urgency. What the system never does automatically is close a case as a duplicate without telling its reporter which problem it was joined to.

### Safety

Three separate controls, because they defend against three different failures.

**Instructions hidden in inbound text.** Every inbound email and forum post is untrusted data. The step that reads it and drafts the case has read-only tools and cannot send anything. Sending is a separate step that only uses fixed kinds of message (a clarifying question, an acknowledgement, a fix notice, an approved answer), so a message cannot trick the agent into sending something else or writing to someone else. This follows the OWASP guidance and the "lethal trifecta" analysis: containment should not depend on the model spotting an attack ([OWASP LLM01](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm01-prompt-injection/)).

**Safeguarding and data rights skip the AI.** A classifier and keyword filter run before any drafting. Anything that looks like abuse, a threat, self-harm, a safeguarding concern, a request to see or delete personal data, or a complaint to the ICO goes straight to a named human with a clock started. A subject access request can use any wording on any channel, so this filter errs towards catching too much.

**Account data only to the right person.** The tool that reads account data returns what the verified reporter is entitled to see. For a volunteer reporting about a member in their own group, that is what ModTools would show them. For a member, it is their own data only. Anything about a third party is used to understand the case but not quoted back.

### Evolving the FSM rather than replacing it

The FSM keeps its loop, its Discourse watcher, its fixer and its reviewer. What changes is where its verdicts go (the case store), what it can see (the Helper's member lookup, so it can resolve "which member" itself), and what it asks (the batched checklist question rather than the current free-form one). The seven sweep rules still living only in prompts become code, and the FSM gets a positive heartbeat: "processed N topics, asked M questions, opened K pull requests in the last hour", checked by the status container, so the silent failures above become visible within an hour instead of when someone notices.

The Helper gets the router its July plan proposed. Member investigations stay as they are. Bug reports go to the case store for the FSM. Product questions get an answer drafted for approval. This is the cheapest route to the plan's own finding that most Helper traffic was not what it was built for.

### Closing the loop

When a pull request that references a problem is merged and deployed, the problem is marked fixed and every linked case gets a short notice in the channel it came from: the fixed "please retest" post on Discourse, a plain email for members, and an in-app notice where there is one. The FSM already posts the Discourse version; the change is that one fix now notifies everyone who reported the same problem, not just the topic it was found in. A weekly "fixed this week" post on Discourse covers volunteers who did not report but were affected, and gives a fallback when email does not arrive.

### Testing before anything goes live

The 663 historical reports are the test set. Before any change goes live it is replayed against them: does it pick the same kind, would it have asked the right question, what tier does it give, which problem does it join. The nine misjudged cases from the May sweep are the first fixed tests, and every future human override of an agent decision is added as another. The measures that matter are how often a human overrides a decision, how often a case is reopened, how many questions a case needed before it was actionable, how long until it was actionable, and how many reporters drop out without answering. Research warns against treating a very low hand-off rate as success, since it can mean the agent is failing to escalate.

### Costs

At about 130 cases a month, each needing a handful of model calls to draft, compare and write, the model cost is a few pounds to a few tens of pounds a month, depending on the model, and embeddings are negligible. This is an estimate from the volumes above, not a measurement. It compares with roughly $0.99 per resolved conversation plus seats for Intercom Fin, or tens of thousands of dollars a year for Zendesk at a larger team size. The real cost is developer time, which is why the phases below are each useful on their own.

### Phased plan

| Phase | Work | Useful on its own because |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Replay harness over the 663 reports; the nine sweep cases as fixed tests; FSM heartbeat | every later change can be measured, and silent failure stops |
| 1 | Case tables in the main database by migration; import `discourse_bug`; FSM writes verdicts there; kind and status separate | one queryable record of every report, visible to more than one machine |
| 2 | The seven prompt-only sweep rules and the urgency rubric enforced in code | fewer wrong "fixed" verdicts; urgency with reasons |

## Conclusion

The difficulty Freegle has had is not a missing ticket system. Its reports arrive incomplete, from two very different kinds of reporter, about people other than the reporter, and the systems it has built so far either could not ask follow-up questions well (the FSM), or could look everything up but only for the one kind of request it was built for (the Helper). The research is clear on the parts that matter: draft from what is known before asking, ask everything in one message, stop asking after a fixed point and proceed, judge urgency against a written rubric, and put anything irreversible or sensitive behind a person while everything else runs on timers. No product sold today does this for a self-hosted Discourse forum and a member database only Freegle's code can read, so the recommendation is to build it, as one shared case store in the main database that both existing systems feed, starting with the test harness that shows whether each step actually helps.

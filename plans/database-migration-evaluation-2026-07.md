# Freegle database migration: evaluation and recommendation

Date: 31 July 2026. Research basis: live production DB inspection (read-only, via the apiv2-live tunnel and SSH), codebase audits of iznik-server-go and iznik-batch, schema-change history audit, and web research with adversarial fact-checking of every load-bearing claim against primary sources (URLs at the end).

## 1. Summary

**Recommendation: migrate to PostgreSQL 18 with PostGIS, run as a two-node Patroni cluster (primary plus hot standby) with a lightweight etcd witness on an existing host. But do it in two stages, and do the cheap urgent things first.**

- **Now (weeks):** upgrade the cluster in place from PXC 8.0.45 to PXC 8.4. Our 8.0 line is already end-of-life (final release 8.0.46-38 shipped 23 July 2026; free upstream security patches have stopped). This closes the security exposure regardless of the long-term choice and removes all time pressure from the bigger decision. At the same time: fix db3's mysqld (it is not under systemd control today, which quietly breaks our rolling-patch story), and raise db2's buffer pool from 6GB, since it is saturated while 17GB of RAM sits unused.
- **Now (parallel, on the current database):** start the raw-SQL-to-ORM rewrite you suggested, mechanical tier first, behind a verification harness (details in section 7). On a Postgres target this is genuine migration de-risking, not just code hygiene, because all that raw SQL has to change dialect anyway.
- **Then (roughly 4 to 8 months):** the PostgreSQL migration proper, gated on a spatial/KNN benchmark proof-of-concept.

Why PostgreSQL: it is the only option that actually fixes both of the two hard technical requirements. Its DDL is transactional and mostly metadata-only (no cluster-wide stall, no full-table rebuilds for the common cases), and PostGIS gives true index-accelerated nearest-neighbour, which neither MySQL nor MariaDB has at all. MariaDB was evaluated seriously and is a lateral move: verified against its own documentation, it fixes neither the DDL pain nor spatial, and is a spatial regression in places.

Honest framing on cost: hosting drops from £5,040/yr to roughly £3,700 to £4,000/yr all-in for the recommended sizing (about £1,050 to £1,350/yr saved, more with tighter sizing). The engineering effort is large, roughly 37 to 43 person-weeks. The hosting saving alone does not pay for this project; the justification is the schema-change pain (which bit us 37 times in the last six months), the spatial ceiling, EOL exposure, and long-term ecosystem health.

## 2. What is actually happening to Galera and MySQL (verified)

The "Galera is being retired in MySQL" story is real but narrower than the headlines:

- MariaDB plc acquired Codership (the Galera authors) in May 2025. On 1 December 2025 it announced that **"MySQL Galera Cluster", the upstream Galera build for plain MySQL, reaches end-of-life on 30 September 2026.** New Galera-family development is being steered towards MariaDB's proprietary Enterprise/Advanced Cluster products. In March 2026 MariaDB plc even tried to strip Galera out of MariaDB Community Server 12.3, then reversed after community backlash; as recently as 30 July 2026 The Register was still questioning Galera's open-source future.
- **None of this kills Percona XtraDB Cluster.** Percona maintains its own Galera fork (long-standing practice), publicly recommitted to PXC on 30 April 2026 with a 3 to 5 year horizon, is shipping PXC 8.4 on a normal cadence (8.4.10-10 on 27 July 2026), and plans PXC on MySQL 9.7 LTS (which Oracle released 21 April 2026).
- **What is genuinely dead is our specific version.** MySQL 8.0 community support ended 30 April 2026, and PXC 8.0.46-38 (23 July 2026) is confirmed as the final PXC 8.0 release. We run 8.0.45-36.1. Percona's only continuation for 8.0 is a paid post-EOL support product; community users get source-only quarterly drops.
- Background risk worth knowing: Oracle laid off around 70 core MySQL engineers in September 2025 and folded the team into the HeatWave unit; a June 2026 governance reform added a steering committee but Percona's co-founder calls it "nothing binding". Both upstreams that the MySQL world depends on (Oracle for the server, MariaDB plc for Galera) have shown cracks within 15 months. Percona is currently absorbing both well, but that is a single-vendor concentration risk over a 3 to 5 year horizon. PostgreSQL has no equivalent concentration risk.

## 3. What we measured (our actual situation)

**Cluster and data.** Percona XtraDB Cluster 8.0.45-36.1, 3 nodes, healthy (Synced/Primary, low flow control). 137GB InnoDB across 231 tables. Biggest tables: rippling_reach 17.1GB (geometry blobs), messages_likes 14.1GB (83M rows), chat_messages 10.3GB (36M rows), bounces 7.6GB, messages 7.6GB, logs 5.6GB (42M rows), email_tracking_images 5.4GB (52M rows), paf_addresses 5.3GB (73M rows). Write stream averages ~380 UPDATEs/sec plus ~34 INSERTs/sec. Peak connections 306. No fulltext indexes anywhere; 10 spatial indexes across 9 tables.

**Topology (fresh read-only SSH inspection, 31 July 2026; full command evidence in the audit transcript).** Writes go to **db3** (12 vCPU/35GB, buffer pool raised 6GB to 16GB on 17 July). Reads go to **db2** (8 vCPU/23GB, buffer pool still 6GB, mysqld at ~569% of 800% CPU: genuinely saturated, and swapping). **db1** is a near-idle standby whose job is the nightly XtraBackup hot stream to gs://freegle_backup_uk (it desyncs during the backup; nothing kept locally). Three operational landmines, independent of any vendor story:

1. db3's mysqld is **not systemd-managed** (started manually with --wsrep-new-cluster; systemctl reports "failed" while it runs). Our rolling-patch plan assumes systemctl works on the write node. It does not, today.
2. **No node has disk room for a second copy of the data** (163GB datadir, 62 to 88GB free per node; db3 is 78% full). Any side-by-side migration needs new storage.
3. **Nothing enforces the read/write split.** No MySQL-protocol proxy exists; the split is .env-file IP wiring edited by hand (17 dated backup copies of the env files show the write IP flipping back and forth), and all three nodes have read_only=OFF.

**Schema-change pain, quantified.** 117 genuine schema changes since December 2025, 92% of them in the last six months (roughly 18 per month during the Rippling build-out). **37 of the 117 (32%) needed a hand-written manual SQL runbook** because plain `artisan migrate` was judged unsafe against live Galera. One change had no online path at all and needed a bespoke ~200-line online-schema-change Artisan command (MigrateReachBoundsSchemaCommand) just to add a NOT NULL geometry column. Galera's TOI (total order isolation) stalls writes cluster-wide for the duration of any DDL regardless of MySQL's own INSTANT/INPLACE classification. Concrete absurdity: chat_messages is ROW_FORMAT=COMPRESSED, which disqualifies ALGORITHM=INSTANT, so we renamed a dead column rather than add one to our second-biggest table. This is a monthly operational tax, not an annual annoyance.

**Spatial, and two data landmines.** The delicate spatial SQL is not in "the spatial server": iznik-spatial-go (the KNN sidecar) runs against its own SQLite R-tree, rebuilt nightly from six MySQL tables, and exists precisely because MySQL cannot do index-accelerated KNN; iznik-routing-go (isochrones) talks to Percona directly via 61 bare database/sql call sites. The real geometry SQL is spread across ~28 Go files and ~19 PHP files. Two facts from the code's own comments matter enormously for migration:

1. **~94% of stored rippling_reach polygons are technically invalid geometry** (ReachBoundsService.php:23, ExpandService.php:61). MySQL tolerates this. PostGIS will not, without a bulk ST_MakeValid pass.
2. **Every geometry column is labelled SRID 3857 (Web Mercator, metres) but actually stores WGS84 lat/lng degrees** (config/freegle.php:335, utils.go:137, ExpandService.php comments). MySQL mostly ignores the mismatch; PostGIS enforces SRID semantics. Fix is a metadata-only relabel to 4326 at import (same as the ST_SRID relabel pattern DoogalService.php already uses), not a reprojection, but it must be planned, and the degree-tuned buffer/simplify tolerances re-checked.

**Raw SQL surface.** iznik-server-go: ~1,677 raw SQL sites in production code, versus only ~140 idiomatic GORM chains (about 90% raw). A hand-read sample suggests ~75% are simple mechanical single-table operations (raw by habit, not necessity). iznik-batch: ~480 business-logic raw sites (excluding migration DDL and vendor code), skewed the other way: over half complex, concentrated in delicate services (UnifiedDigestService, StatsGenerationService, NewsfeedDigestService, ExpandService, GroupStatsService). MySQL-specific upsert idioms are pervasive: ~154 ON DUPLICATE KEY UPDATE, ~78 INSERT IGNORE, ~32 REPLACE INTO in Go alone. None of that syntax exists on Postgres. (The sample-based proportions should be firmed up with a scripted inventory as the first step of the rewrite; see section 7.)

## 4. The options, scored

Four serious options. Group Replication / InnoDB Cluster (staying MySQL with Oracle's native clustering) was also evaluated and rejected: it fixes neither DDL nor spatial, several of its documented limits (9-member cap, transaction size ceilings) apply in single-primary mode too, Percona's benchmark shows its throughput collapsing ~75% when configured for Galera-equivalent consistency, and it trades Percona-dependence for dependence on Oracle's recently destabilised MySQL org.

| Requirement | A. Stay Percona: PXC 8.4 | B. MariaDB Galera 11.8 LTS | C. PostgreSQL 18 + Patroni |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixes EOL exposure | **Yes.** Supported line, MySQL 9.7 LTS next. In-place, node-by-node rolling upgrade within the Percona family | Yes, but the migration is a full logical dump/reload (no in-place path from MySQL 8.0's data dictionary), with collation (utf8mb4_0900_*), JSON and auth-plugin fixups | Yes (entirely new engine) |
| Rolling OS patches, no outage | Yes (Galera rolling restarts), once db3's systemd gap is fixed | Same as A | Yes, via Patroni switchover; automatic failover realistically 25 to 45 seconds with sensible tuning (not the "1 to 2s" sometimes quoted), so client retry logic must cover that window |
| Online schema changes | **Unchanged: poor.** Same TOI stalls, same rebuild classes. pt-online-schema-change/gh-ost remain workarounds with caveats | **Unchanged: poor, verified.** MariaDB's INSTANT/NOCOPY excludes exactly the ALTERs that hurt us (type changes, PK changes, ROW_FORMAT, SPATIAL index on populated table); TOI unchanged | **Fixed for the cases that matter.** Metadata-only ADD COLUMN with default (since PG11), CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY, NOT VALID + VALIDATE CONSTRAINT, pg_repack for genuine rewrites. Transactional DDL, no cluster-wide barrier |
| Spatial including KNN | Unchanged: workable via our MBR-prefilter pattern and the KNN sidecar; no true KNN (MySQL bug #101649 open since 2020) | **Regression.** ST_Contains/ST_Within/ST_Area are Euclidean-only regardless of SRID; no ST_MakeValid; still no KNN | **Best for the in-database spatial SQL** (GiST KNN via the <-> operator, the exact ORDER BY distance LIMIT N pattern MySQL cannot index; ST_MakeValid fixes the 94%-invalid corpus natively). The KNN sidecar stays as-is: it is heavily custom, SQLite-backed and decoupled, and only its nightly rebuild source queries repoint at the new database |
| Background vs API read isolation | Preserved as today (3 nodes), or degraded to shared roles at 2-node+garbd | Same as A | MVCC removes the locking rationale entirely (readers never block writers); resource isolation via pgbouncer role pools + statement_timeout/work_mem; the dedicated background node runs at only ~25% CPU today, so a shared standby is plausible but should be piloted |
| Hosting cost (compute) | £420/mo as-is; **£240/mo possible** with 2 nodes + garbd arbitrator (garbd is an official stateless Galera quorum component, works with PXC) | £420/mo at parity; £240/mo with 2 nodes + garbd | **£300/mo recommended** (ROCK-36 primary + ROCK-24 standby + free witness); £240/mo variant possible |
| Migration effort | **Near zero** (rolling upgrade + regression test) | Moderate (dump/reload 137GB, dialect nearly unchanged) | **Large: ~37 to 43 person-weeks** (dialect port of ~1,600+ raw Go sites and the hard Laravel services, spatial data cleaning, new ops stack) |
| Ecosystem longevity | Good near-term (Percona solid); 3-5 year concentration risk on Percona's fork, atop Oracle's wobbles | Weakest: Galera's openness contested twice in six months; plc steering cluster tech proprietary | Strongest: community-governed, no vendor to lose |
| Ops complexity for a tiny team | Familiar | Familiar | Genuinely new discipline (autovacuum, WAL/replication-slot monitoring, Patroni/etcd, pgBackRest) plus training/consultancy cost |

**Conclusion:** A (PXC 8.4) is the mandatory immediate step and the fallback destination; it is cheap, safe, and solves the EOL problem, but leaves both stated pain points unsolved. B (MariaDB) is strictly dominated: all of A's limitations, plus a real migration, plus spatial regressions, plus the shakiest vendor story; rejected. C (PostgreSQL) is the only option that answers the brief, at a real effort cost. Hence the two-stage recommendation: do A now, then do C deliberately.

The 2-node+garbd variant of A is worth knowing about as a pure cost lever (£2,160/yr saved with near-zero effort) if we ever wanted savings without the Postgres project, with two caveats: during any node-down window the surviving node is a zero-redundancy single point of failure with SST/IST donor load landing on an already-hot fleet, and it loses the dedicated backup node, which is the whole reason backups do not hurt us today (see "Backups in a two-node world" in section 5; preserving backup isolation costs ~£90/mo and roughly halves the saving).

## 5. Recommended target architecture (PostgreSQL)

- **Nodes:** primary ROCK-36 (12 vCPU/36GB/300GB NVMe, £180/mo), hot standby ROCK-24 (8 vCPU/24GB/200GB, £120/mo, may need ~100GB extra block storage at £0.15/GB/mo). Standby serves background/batch reads, mirroring today's split. Asymmetric sizing is deliberate; the failover runbook notes that a failed-over primary runs on the smaller box until resized (a quick cloud resize).
- **HA:** Patroni + etcd, chosen over repmgr and pg_auto_failover deliberately: repmgr is simpler on day one but is in low-maintenance mode upstream and its custom quorum model has a documented production split-brain history, while Patroni's DCS-lease design rules that state out by construction and is actively maintained; both need a witness node anyway, so the simplicity gap disappears in an HA deployment (detail in section 10). Use etcd for the DCS (Patroni's production-ready backend; its pure-Raft mode is flagged deprecated/beta upstream). The etcd witness (a lightweight voting process, no Postgres, no data) goes on the ha HAProxy VM rather than the app/docker host, so DB quorum does not share fate with the app stack. Enable Patroni's watchdog (softdog) for self-fencing to prevent split-brain. Tune primary_start_timeout down from its 300s default; target RTO under 2 minutes for automatic failover, RPO a few seconds (async) with synchronous_commit=remote_apply opt-in per write path where read-your-writes matters (never globally at ~380 writes/sec).
- **Routing:** vip-manager (floating IP follows the Patroni leader key), so applications keep a single write address; Laravel's native read/write config and GORM dbresolver carry over as config changes. One spike required first: dbresolver's SELECT-sniffing against a Postgres dialector has an unresolved upstream issue report (go-gorm/gorm#5010), and Postgres-idiomatic `WITH ... SELECT` and `INSERT ... RETURNING` must be proven to route correctly. This finally gives us DB-side enforcement the current setup lacks (standby is genuinely read-only in Postgres).
- **Connections:** pgbouncer in transaction pooling mode is mandatory (Postgres is process-per-connection; today's 306 peak connections must map to well under ~150 real backends), with separate pools and per-role statement_timeout/work_mem for frontend vs batch. Audit for session-level SET and server-side prepared statements first (transaction pooling breaks both).
- **Backup:** pgBackRest, full + incremental + WAL archive, to UK/EU-resident S3-compatible storage (Krystal object storage, roughly £7 to £15/mo; the data includes PII, so bucket residency is a GDPR decision, not just cost). Guard against replication-slot WAL bloat with max_slot_wal_keep_size and disk alerting from day one (a new failure mode with no Galera analogue, and our disks are tight). Keep the existing XtraBackup pipeline running until well past cutover; at least one full pgBackRest restore drill is a go/no-go gate.
- **Monitoring:** pg_stat_statements + postgres_exporter; new dashboards for replication lag and transaction-ID wraparound age (autovacuum is not set-and-forget the way InnoDB purge mostly is).
- **Collation and encoding:** UTF8 handles 4-byte emoji natively (no MySQL utf8mb3 trap). The schema-wide case-insensitive collation is the subtle one: target PG18 specifically because LIKE now works with nondeterministic ICU collations; ILIKE still does not, so the hard-site rewrite decides per column between a nondeterministic collation and citext/lower() indexes, and audits every ILIKE.

### Backups in a two-node world (the "backup interferes with traffic" problem)

Today backups are safe purely by architecture: db1 is a dedicated third node that desyncs from the cluster and streams XtraBackup nightly, so backup I/O and backup locking never touch a serving node. We have seen what happens otherwise: MySQL's backup takes instance-level backup locks (LOCK INSTANCE FOR BACKUP plus a brief log lock) that can block behind, or hold up, DDL and long-running statements, and the backup's disk/CPU load competes with queries. Any two-node topology has to answer this explicitly.

**PostgreSQL two-node answer (good, and a genuine point in its favour):** pgBackRest supports backup-from-standby (`backup-standby=y`): the base backup is a file-level copy taken from the **standby**, with only brief coordination against the primary, so the primary never carries backup I/O. There is no FTWRL-style lock anywhere in the process: `pg_backup_start` is non-blocking, file copying takes no SQL locks, and MVCC means neither reads nor writes on either node are blocked by the backup, nor can a long-running query block the backup. Continuous WAL archiving runs alongside, which also upgrades us from "nightly full" to point-in-time recovery. The backup load lands on the standby, which also serves batch reads, so: schedule the weekly full plus daily incrementals off-peak (incrementals are cheap), cap backup parallelism (`process-max`) and compression level if contention appears, and watch batch-query latency during the first backup cycles. Monthly restore drills into the dev/yesterday environment. Net: two nodes suffice for Postgres without recreating today's third box, because the standby we already pay for doubles as the backup source without locking anybody.

**MySQL-family 2-node+garbd answer (weak, and the honest reason that option is cheaper than it looks):** the nightly xtrabackup would have to run on the read node with wsrep_desync=ON, which means one node effectively serves everything for the whole backup window, backup I/O rides on a serving node, and the historical lock-interference risks return. A node failure during the window is an outage. The clean fix is a small async replica (a plain binlog replica off the cluster, not a Galera member) as the dedicated backup source: roughly a ROCK-12 plus ~200GB block storage, about £90/mo, which erodes the 2-node saving from £180/mo to roughly £90/mo. That asymmetry (Postgres gets safe backups from a node we already pay for; two-node MySQL needs an extra box to keep backups safe) is itself part of the case for the Postgres topology.

## 7. The raw-SQL-to-ORM rewrite: a locked-in process

Yes to the rewrite, as a hybrid (ORM for the mechanical majority, a small explicit raw layer for the hard sites), starting now on the current database. The design goal of the process below is that **missing a site or silently deferring one is structurally impossible**: the work is driven by a machine-generated inventory that CI enforces, not by anyone's diligence. Past migrations went wrong exactly where human tracking was the mechanism; this removes human tracking from the loop.

### 7.1 The inventory is the contract

1. **Build an extractor** (checked in, e.g. `tools/orm-migration/extract.mjs`) that mechanically finds every raw SQL call site: iznik-server-go (`.Raw(`, `.Exec(` on any receiver), iznik-batch (`DB::select/statement/insert/update/delete/raw`, `whereRaw`, `selectRaw`, `orderByRaw`, `havingRaw`), and iznik-routing-go (`database/sql` Query/QueryRow/Prepare/Exec). For each site it records: a stable ID (hash of path plus normalised SQL), file:line, enclosing function, statement kind, tables touched, MySQL-isms present (backticks, ON DUPLICATE KEY, ANY_VALUE, DATE_FORMAT, TIMESTAMPDIFF, IFNULL, FORCE INDEX, ST_*/MBR*, LAST_INSERT_ID), and a complexity class.
2. **The extractor output is committed as `manifest.json`.** Every site carries exactly one status: `raw`, `in-progress`, `converted`, `keep-raw` (with a written reason), `test-fixture`, or `retired`.
3. **CI ratchet (gate 1), on every PR:** re-run the extractor and fail the build if (a) the code contains any raw site not in the manifest, so new raw SQL cannot be added except deliberately, with a status and reason; (b) any site's SQL changed without its manifest entry being updated in the same PR; or (c) the count of `raw` plus `in-progress` sites increased. Because the inventory is generated from the code it is exhaustive by construction, and the ratchet forbids regression. "We missed a bit" cannot happen; "we quietly deferred a bit" cannot happen either, because deferral requires an explicit `keep-raw` entry with a reason that survives review.

### 7.2 The harness comes first: all four layers exist before the first conversion merges

- **Layer 1, golden-SQL parity (every converted site, no exceptions).** The original raw SQL string is stored in the manifest as the golden. A parity test (Go: GORM `Session{DryRun}` / `ToSQL`; Laravel: `toRawSql()`) renders the new ORM call with fixed representative bind values and asserts canonicalised equality with the golden: lowercase keywords, collapsed whitespace, quote-style normalisation, alias renaming, with both sides passed through the same transform. Where the ORM legitimately emits different-but-equivalent SQL (say an explicit column list instead of `*`), the reviewer records the approved diff and a justification in the manifest entry; CI fails on any unapproved divergence. **Gate 2: a site cannot be marked `converted` unless a parity test bearing its ID exists and passes.** The extractor checks test existence mechanically.
- **Layer 2, result parity on fixtures (every converted site).** A test runs the old raw SQL and the new ORM call against the seeded test database and asserts identical result sets (order-sensitive where the query has ORDER BY, sorted comparison otherwise, NULL-sensitive). This catches what string comparison cannot: collation, NULL handling, implicit-cast semantics.
- **Layer 3, shadow execution in production (read sites, per-batch).** Each conversion batch ships behind a feature flag. While the flag is on in shadow mode, the harness runs the old query, serves the old result, runs the new query asynchronously, compares row hashes, and logs any divergence to Loki with the site ID; a Grafana panel alerts on divergence above zero. A batch's flag moves to "new live" only after a soak at zero divergence: 48 hours minimum, one full week for hot paths, chosen so weekly batch jobs execute at least once. Prerequisite: the db2 buffer pool fix in section 8, so the temporarily doubled read load is safe.
- **Layer 4, write parity by replay (write sites; writes are never shadow-run live).** Use the existing "yesterday" restore system as the replay substrate: restore the snapshot twice, run the old write path against copy A and the new against copy B with identical inputs (driven by that site's integration test), and diff the affected tables. Plus `EXPLAIN FORMAT=TREE` plan-parity in CI for every site tagged hot in the manifest, and one integration test per upsert site per dialect, because of two verified traps: GORM `clause.OnConflict` mishandles WHERE clauses on the conflict target for some drivers (go-gorm/gorm#4355, go-gorm/mysql#39), and Laravel's MySQL driver silently ignores `upsert()`'s uniqueBy argument while Postgres requires it to name a real unique index, so an upsert correct on MySQL can be silently wrong on Postgres when a table has several unique indexes.

### 7.3 Conversion waves, fixed order, small batches

- **Wave 0, pilot:** ten sites of mixed shape taken end-to-end through all four layers, to prove the pipeline and calibrate per-site cost. Nothing else converts until the pilot retrospective.
- **Wave 1:** single-table SELECT with simple predicates. **Wave 2:** single-table INSERT/UPDATE/DELETE. **Wave 3:** upserts, through the portable wrappers (GORM `clause.OnConflict`, Laravel `Model::upsert()`), which emit ON DUPLICATE KEY UPDATE on MySQL and ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE on Postgres from one call site. **Wave 4:** multi-table SELECTs containing no MySQL-only functions. **Wave 5:** triage of everything left into `keep-raw` (with reason) or an individually planned conversion.
- **Batch rules:** at most ~20 sites per PR, one module or package per PR, so review is real; a PR merges only with layers 1 and 2 green (plus 4 for writes) and the full existing suites green; layer-3 soak completes before the next batch touches the same module; a wave does not start until the previous wave's raw count is zero for the active codebase. Each PR is a pure no-behaviour-change release.
- The burn-down (manifest status counts over time) is a dashboard, so progress and any stall are visible, not anecdotal.

### 7.4 Definition of done: no deferred bits

The programme is complete when the manifest contains zero `raw` and zero `in-progress` entries. At that point every site is exactly one of: `converted` (with its parity tests), `keep-raw` (with a written reason, and a per-dialect porting note: this is the explicit, bounded hard-site list, expected around 50 to 80 sites, which becomes the input to migration Phase 4), `test-fixture` (see 7.6), or `retired` (deleted). A one-page completion report lists every keep-raw site and its reason. There is no residual category for anything to hide in.

### 7.5 What stays raw, by design

The genuinely hard sites where forcing the ORM would hurt readability and a rewrite bug would be silent and expensive. Named examples found by the audits: `message/bounds.go` `Bounds()` (two 20-line spatial/CASE/ANY_VALUE queries; a bug here silently shows wrong nearby posts), `utils/reachcap.go` `AuthorReachCapWhere` (the most widely reused WHERE fragment in the codebase: JSON extraction plus haversine trig plus spatial), `RestoreUserCommand`'s dynamic `upsertRow` helper (needs per-table redesign because ON CONFLICT requires a named constraint), the `ExpandService`/`ReachBoundsService` polygon bookkeeping, and the stored functions. These live behind a thin repository layer so the dialect-specific SQL is in one findable place per site, each with its own integration test.

### 7.6 Test-fixture SQL, stated honestly

The Go test files contain roughly 4,000 further raw SQL usages (fixture setup). They are out of scope for ORM-ification, since they are not production queries, but they are not ignored: the manifest tags them `test-fixture`, and they are in scope for the engine migration (they will not run on Postgres as written). They get budgeted in migration Phases 4 to 5, not rediscovered at cutover.

### 7.7 Effort and sequencing

Extractor, manifest and CI ratchet: about 1 week. Harness layers 1 to 4: about 2 weeks. Pilot: 1 week. Waves 1 to 4 across both codebases: about 4 to 6 weeks given the measured proportions (~75% of the ~1,677 Go sites are mechanical; Laravel's mechanical share is smaller). Wave 5 triage: days. Total roughly 9 to 11 person-weeks, the first three of which are prerequisites for everything else. It all runs on the current MySQL database, and it is worth doing even if the Postgres migration were later cancelled: for MariaDB or PXC it is code quality and portability; for Postgres it is critical-path de-risking, because every one of those raw strings has to change dialect anyway.

## 8. Immediate actions (do these regardless, roughly 1 to 2 person-weeks total)

1. Put db3's mysqld back under systemd control (currently a hand-bootstrapped process; systemctl thinks it is failed).
2. Raise db2's innodb_buffer_pool_size from 6GB towards ~16GB (matching db3's July change); it is the saturated node and has 17GB of RAM idle. This may materially relieve today's read-node pain on its own, and makes the shadow-testing load in section 7 safe to run.
3. Plan and execute the rolling upgrade PXC 8.0.45 to 8.4 (node at a time; verify with Percona's upgrade runbook; regression-test on a dev restore first). Alternative if we want to defer even that: Percona's paid MySQL 8.0 post-EOL support as a bridge.
4. Document the backup pipeline and test a restore (it is currently a single undocumented script on db1).

## 9. Radical options (evaluated per your brief)

- **Push more writes into background processing: adopt now, selectively.** Defer high-volume, low-criticality writes (like counters, email tracking hits, search logging, stats) through the existing Laravel queue/batch layer instead of synchronous API-path writes. Engine-agnostic, can start on MySQL today, and under Postgres it directly reduces autovacuum/WAL churn (fewer, larger transactions), which is our main new operational risk. Never for anything gating moderation or safety decisions.
- **Front-end reads from a cache, not full SQL: adopt now, in a narrow form.** Full event-sourced CQRS is rejected: disproportionate for a two-person ops team and creates an unowned projection pipeline to fail silently. The right-sized version is a Redis/Valkey cache for the two or three hottest read paths (browse/nearby feed, group listings), invalidated inline by our only two writers (apiv2, batch). No event bus required.
- **Telemetry offload: adopt later, after the engine migration settles, but with DuckDB rather than ClickHouse (the greenfield review in section 10 changed this call).** messages_likes, bounces, logs, email_tracking*, stats, users_searches are ~40 to 43GB of append/scan telemetry. Offloading shrinks OLTP to roughly 95 to 100GB (not 60 to 80GB: rippling_reach is live application state, not telemetry). Worthwhile, but do not stack two architecture changes at once.
- **Rejected:** TiDB (no spatial support at all; see the spatial caveat in section 10's ScyllaDB entry, which weakens this), Vitess (solves a sharding problem we do not have), CockroachDB (interesting DDL story but a bigger rewrite than Postgres, partial PostGIS compatibility, and free-tier telemetry-lapse throttling; revisit only if ever needed), ReadySet (BSL licence caps free production use at 2GB RAM; commercial pricing sales-gated), PolyScale (dead, dissolved 2024), Heimdall (opaque, duplicates what we built), AWS RDS/Aurora and Supabase (2 to 3x current spend once HA-sized, plus cross-provider latency/egress against app servers staying at Krystal), Hetzner dedicated (post June-2026 repricing it is ~4x cheaper, not 7 to 10x, has no UK datacentre, and adds cross-provider DB latency; not worth it), SQLite/LiteFS/Turso/DuckDB (wrong shape entirely).

## 10. If we were starting today: would we even use a relational database?

The founder's question: a few years ago we might have gone NoSQL; what are the cool kids doing nowadays? Researched July 2026 (four angles, each adversarially fact-checked; sources inline).

### The short answer

Yes, we would still be relational, and it is not close. Every piece of 2025-2026 evidence points the same way: Postgres, extended with PostGIS and pgvector, is what a competent team builds a join-heavy, consistency-sensitive, spatially-aware application on today. That is, point for point, the migration already recommended in this document. The memory that "a few years ago we might have gone NoSQL" is accurate: the industry did go through that wave, and it has largely reversed since. Boring, but boring is the honest answer.

### What the 2026 zeitgeist actually is

The clearest single data point is Stack Overflow's 2025 developer survey: PostgreSQL usage jumped to 55.6% (58.2% among professional developers), the largest single-year rise in the survey's history, topping "most used", "most admired" (65.5%) and "most desired" (46.5%) for the third or fourth year running (vonng.com/en/pg/so2025-pg). MongoDB was the only major database to shrink year on year (roughly 24.8% down to 24.0%), sliding from the #1 most-wanted database in 2017-2020 to #5 by 2025. MongoDB the company is not dying (Atlas revenue is growing in the high-20s to 30% range and it turned its first profitable quarter in Q1 FY2027); the split is that existing customers are moving to managed Atlas while developers choosing a database for new work choose Postgres.

The strongest "what are the cool kids doing" signal is what gets picked when nobody is choosing at all, because an AI coding agent picks for them. Supabase (Postgres-as-backend-platform) reports AI tools now start over 60% of its new databases, with ARR from about $101M to $170M in five months and a $10.5B valuation in June 2026 (cnbc.com/2026/06/04); Databricks bought Neon (serverless Postgres) for about $1B, and over 80% of new Neon databases are created by AI agents; Vercel ships Postgres as its default. With no legacy and no bias in the room, the default is Postgres.

At real scale the pattern holds: Figma has scaled Postgres roughly 100x since 2020 (sharding plus a custom query router, still investing as of May 2026, figma.com/blog/pgkeeper). Nextdoor, the closest product comparison to Freegle in this whole exercise, runs Postgres as its system of record with Valkey purely as a look-aside cache in front of it (engblog.nextdoor.com), not the other way round.

There is credible pushback worth taking seriously: the widely-read "just use Postgres" debate (532 points, 333 comments, tigerdata.com/blog/its-2026-just-use-postgres) surfaces the real point that Postgres needs genuine operational care (vacuum, reindexing, careful upgrades), though one prominent dissenter there is a ClickHouse employee with a commercial stake in the alternative. The honest middle ground, adopted here: Postgres as the default, a specialised store only once you hit a measured limit, not because of a conference talk.

### NoSQL, assessed honestly against Freegle's shape

- **Document stores (MongoDB).** Genuinely good for schema-volatile, low-join, narrow-access-pattern data: content platforms, IoT, variable-field catalogues. Freegle is the opposite shape: users, memberships, groups, messages, chats, moderation state and spam checks join constantly, and ad-hoc cross-entity queries are daily moderator and support work. More decisive: Mongo's 2dsphere geo index does containment and proximity but has no polygon union, intersection, difference or repair, so it cannot do the reach and isochrone work our PostGIS layer does. Plain no.
- **Wide-column (Cassandra, ScyllaDB, DynamoDB, Firestore).** These win for one dominant, frozen, write-firehose access pattern at extreme scale; Discord's message store (trillions of rows, no joins, no ad-hoc queries, discord.com/blog/how-discord-stores-trillions-of-messages) is the textbook case. We are three orders of magnitude smaller and need exactly the ad-hoc querying this family handles worst. DynamoDB and Firestore also only exist inside AWS and GCP, ruled out by our fixed-cost self-hosted UK model alone. Firm no. (ScyllaDB, the one self-hostable member, is taken individually in the alternatives list below.)
- **In-memory KV (Redis/Valkey).** Cache or queue in front of a durable database, never the system of record: even AWS's June 2026 Valkey durability upgrade accepts up to a 10-second write-loss window in async mode, disqualifying for a moderation audit trail. Use it the way Nextdoor does, as a cache, if and when needed.
- **Graph (Neo4j and friends).** In 2025-2026 production use these are consistently a secondary analytics layer beside a relational system of record, not the record. Our graph-shaped data (user to membership to group, chat participants) is shallow and served by joins and recursive CTEs; PostgreSQL 19 (beta, June 2026) even adds native SQL/PGQ graph syntax on ordinary tables. No case.

### The genuinely new paradigms

Two things in the 2024-2026 window are real novelty rather than repackaged NoSQL:

- **Local-first sync engines** (ElectricSQL, PowerSync, Rocicorp's Zero, which hit 1.0 in June 2026). Real production traction, but every one of them, including Linear's famous custom engine, treats Postgres as the authority and the local layer as a cache (Zero's creator calls it "a fancy cache" that cannot corrupt data). Not a database replacement; a plausible future answer to our chat/notification polling, on top of Postgres.
- **Serverless/managed Postgres** (Neon, Supabase, PlanetScale Postgres). Real Postgres under a different operating model, built for bursty, ephemeral, many-small-database workloads (AI agents spinning up throwaway DBs). An always-on 137GB instance prices out at roughly $350/month on Neon before HA, comparable to or above our two Krystal VMs, and it would move the database off the UK network our app servers live on. Not a fit, but its existence confirms rather than contradicts the Postgres choice.

Everything else surveyed fails on arithmetic, workload shape, licensing or team size: server-side SQLite (Turso, Cloudflare D1) has a contested real-world write ceiling (roughly 200 to 2,000 writes/sec depending on shape) and a single-writer architecture that is a poor match for our ~400 writes/sec join-heavy 137GB profile; CockroachDB retired free self-hosting entirely in November 2024; NewSQL generally solves multi-region write-scale problems we do not have; SurrealDB only made durable commits the default in its 3.0 release in 2026, a maturity flag for a system of record; Gel (formerly EdgeDB) markets itself as "Postgres unchained", which is itself an admission of what the base layer worth building on is.

### The satellites a 2026 greenfield build would attach

This is where the greenfield lens earns its keep: not the core (which it confirms) but what surrounds it. Four researched decisions, with a common theme: at our scale, nearly every "add a new service" temptation loses to "Postgres already does this".

- **Realtime chat/notifications (replacing API polling): LISTEN/NOTIFY plus SSE, no new infrastructure.** One caveat matters and is planned for: LISTEN is incompatible with pgbouncer's transaction pooling (the subscription lives on a backend connection the pooler reassigns; pgbouncer issue #655). The standard pattern is a single long-lived listener process per app node holding one dedicated non-pooled connection to the Patroni primary; writers call pg_notify with the message id inside the same transaction as the write; the listener fans the id out over SSE (simpler than websockets for "something changed, re-fetch" traffic) and clients fetch the row through the normal pooled path. Failover degrades to polling cadence briefly, losing nothing durable. Our volume is far below the ~1,000 notifications/sec soft ceiling. Even Supabase's 2026 realtime rebuild converged on "logical replication plus a thin relay", not Kafka.
- **Search: ParadeDB pg_search (BM25 inside Postgres) is the strong candidate; Meilisearch is the fallback.** We have no fulltext today. Postgres native FTS lacks typo tolerance; OpenSearch is a JVM cluster and the wrong shape for two people. pg_search is production-real in 2026 (native Postgres index type on Tantivy/Rust, 250k+ extension installs, Alibaba Cloud among named users) and would give BM25 plus pgvector hybrid ranking in one SQL query with no second system and no sync lag. The operational asterisk: it is a compiled extension that must be built and pinned alongside PG18 and PostGIS across Patroni nodes and major upgrades; evaluate that during migration Phase 0. If the extension risk reads too high, Meilisearch (single binary, native hybrid search that can consume our existing HuggingFace embeddings) beats Typesense and OpenSearch for our team size.
- **Telemetry analytics: DuckDB, not ClickHouse.** At ~40GB the engines are equivalent in capability, so the decision is ops model: ClickHouse is another whole system with a distributed-system config surface; DuckDB is an in-process binary speaking Postgres-flavoured SQL that reads Postgres and Parquet directly. Either export telemetry to Parquet and query with standalone DuckDB, or run pg_duckdb (1.0, September 2025, production-ready) on a reporting replica so stats dashboards get columnar scans with zero new services. Note ParadeDB's older pg_analytics is archived; pg_duckdb is the maintained embedding.
- **Queues/jobs: keep them in Postgres, on the same instance.** FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED queueing is thoroughly production-proven in 2026: Oban (Elixir) demonstrates ~1M jobs/minute on a single Postgres instance, River (Go, riverqueue.com) is the same architecture and directly relevant to apiv2, and Laravel's own database driver uses the same pattern. Our write rate is 10-40x below those demonstrated ceilings. The transactional-enqueue property (a job exists only if the business transaction committed) is a correctness win a separate Redis-backed queue cannot give. Do not add Valkey for this.

### What this changes in the plan

- **Core recommendation: unchanged and independently reinforced.** PostgreSQL 18 + PostGIS + Patroni is what the 2026 evidence picks for this workload even with no legacy to migrate.
- **During migration:** design connection handling for the realtime listener pattern up front (one non-pooled LISTEN connection coexisting with pgbouncer transaction pooling); evaluate pg_search in Phase 0 alongside the other extension decisions; queues stay in Postgres (no change, and no Valkey).
- **Later:** fold the vector-search half of the embedding sidecar into pgvector/pgvectorscale (our corpus is well under the scale where a dedicated vector store earns its keep); telemetry offload with DuckDB per section 9; a local-first sync-engine pilot (Zero/ElectricSQL/PowerSync) only if we want richer realtime UX than the LISTEN/NOTIFY+SSE step provides.
- **Never:** any NoSQL family as system of record; serverless/managed Postgres as the hosting model; server-side SQLite; NewSQL; multi-model newcomers. Each has a real niche, and none of those niches is our workload.

### Specific alternatives asked about (checked individually, July 2026)

A reader supplied a list of projects; here is where each stands, with later additions appended. Vitess and TiDB were already evaluated and rejected in section 9 (Vitess solves many-shard scaling we do not have, and its one real benefit, online DDL, comes natively with Postgres; TiDB has no spatial support at all, subject to the spatial caveat in the ScyllaDB entry below). Patroni is our actual recommendation. The rest were researched fresh, each claim adversarially checked:

- **OceanBase (oceanbase/oceanbase): reject. Originally on a hard spatial disqualifier; now on ecosystem and operational weight, with spatial demoted (see the caveat at the end of this entry).** MySQL-wire-compatible distributed SQL from Ant/Alibaba, genuinely open (Apache 2.0) and able to scale down to 2 nodes plus an arbitration witness. But OceanBase's own compatibility docs (consistent from 4.0 through the current 4.3.3) state that MySQL mode supports no spatial data types, no spatial functions and no spatial indexes: no GEOMETRY columns, no ST_Contains, no polygon set operations, nothing, and no public roadmap item to add them. In July 2026 that ended the evaluation by itself. Beyond that, the English-language community is thin and the operational model (zones, tenants, resource pools, obproxy, Paxos groups) is far more machinery than two people need for 137GB. **Caveat added later:** the spatial disqualifier is weakening as spatial work moves into `iznik-spatial-go` (measured in the ScyllaDB entry below), so "no spatial" is no longer the one-line end of this evaluation that it was in July 2026. OceanBase still fails on ecosystem thinness and operational weight, which is now the load-bearing half of the rejection.
- **Citus (citusdata/citus): reject for us, without prejudice.** Alive and shipping (14.0 for Postgres 18 in February 2026, 14.1 in July 2026, mostly maintenance releases), and excellent at what it is for: multi-tenant SaaS sharding and columnar analytics. We are a single-tenant 137GB OLTP app, so there is nothing to shard, and worse, spatial is Citus's known weak spot: cross-shard spatial predicates hit unsupported-clause errors and a core maintainer's own explanation on the long-open issue (citusdata/citus#1016) is that geography has no natural sharding dimension ("only 1 contiguous earth"). Also notable: Citus's founding technical lead left in 2024 for Crunchy Data to build a DuckDB-flavoured analytical Postgres, and Crunchy was acquired by Snowflake in June 2025.
- **pgEdge (pgEdge/spock lineage): reject, and for one specific reason worth recording.** Fully open-sourced under the PostgreSQL Licence in September 2025, descended from the 2ndQuadrant BDR/pglogical work, but it is a small project (~740 GitHub stars) whose value is geo-distributed asynchronous multi-master. Its default conflict resolution is last-write-wins: a concurrent write on two nodes silently discards one side with no error, and race divergence needs a separate reconciliation tool. For a moderation system where a hold or approval silently vanishing is a correctness bug, that model is a disqualifier, not a trade-off. A single-region 2-node deployment gains nothing over plain streaming replication anyway.
- **The Kubernetes operators (cloudnative-pg, CrunchyData postgres-operator, StackGres, percona-postgresql-operator, zalando postgres-operator): right tools, wrong prerequisite.** Four of the five are Patroni wrapped in Kubernetes CRDs; CloudNativePG is the exception with its own Kubernetes-native instance manager, and it is winning the ecosystem race (CNCF sandbox January 2025, incubation application November 2025, cncf/toc#1961, roughly 7,700 stars and 132M downloads by end-2025, an Excellent-tier CNCF health score in mid-2026). None of the five runs outside a live Kubernetes cluster, so the real question is whether we would adopt Kubernetes solely to host a two-node database. Practitioner writing from 2024-2026 is consistent: teams without existing Kubernetes operational comfort should not, because it layers a second operational discipline and a four-deep upgrade treadmill (Kubernetes, CSI storage, operator, Postgres) over what two VMs and Patroni do directly, with storage-attach delays on failover as a bonus regression. If we ever run Kubernetes for other reasons, CloudNativePG is the one to pick up; until then, Patroni on VMs is the same automation with one fewer layer.
- **repmgr (a reader runs 1 primary + 1 hot standby on it happily): fine to keep if you have it, not the 2026 default for a new build.** repmgr is not abandoned (EDB still supports it commercially) but it is in low-maintenance mode: last release November 2024, last upstream commit April 2025, with 2026 community PRs sitting unmerged. Patroni ships far more actively (three release branches between April and July 2026). The deeper difference is structural: repmgr's custom quorum/witness model has a documented production split-brain history (GitLab's postmortems, bitnami chart issues), while Patroni's DCS-lease design makes a two-primaries state impossible by construction. Both need a witness node anyway, so repmgr's day-one simplicity advantage mostly evaporates in an HA deployment. The reader's own instinct ("if starting today I might use Patroni") matches the evidence.
- **Plain Percona Server 8.4 (percona/percona-server, no Galera): real option, worse fit for our stated requirement.** Percona packages exactly this (Percona Server + ProxySQL + Orchestrator + XtraBackup and friends), and Orchestrator, after being archived by its original owner in February 2025, was re-homed and is actively developed under ProxySQL as of April 2026. But replacing Galera with async/semi-sync replication swaps our genuinely outage-free rolling restarts for a best-case 10 to 30 second write-unavailability window on every failover, plus the semi-sync-degrades-to-async data-loss edge. Since the whole point of the bridge is "stay patched with no outage while the Postgres migration runs", PXC 8.4 remains the better bridge (with the routing caveat that during a mixed-version rolling upgrade, writes must be directed at the lower-version nodes).
- **ScyllaDB (scylladb/scylladb): reject, but on the data model and the licence, not on spatial.** Covered at family level under wide-column above; named individually because it is the one member of that family the family verdict does not kill on hosting grounds, since unlike DynamoDB and Firestore it self-hosts on our own UK VMs, and being a C++ shared-nothing rewrite of Cassandra rather than a JVM cluster the "wrong shape for two people" objection is weaker than it looks.
  - **Spatial is not the reason, and an earlier draft of this entry was wrong to say it was.** ScyllaDB's geospatial types are DSE-inherited storage and serialisation only, with no predicates, no set operations and no spatial index (scylladb/scylladb#9940, the GIS-index request, has sat open, unassigned and in Backlog since January 2022). That would matter if we still did this work in the database, and we increasingly do not. `iznik-spatial-go` already holds eight datasets in SQLite R-tree indexes and answers KNN, within-polygon, `/containing`, `/reach/admits` and `/groups/intersecting`; its `cellset` package computes the set operations in Go, with the subtraction path documented as "the cell-set equivalent of ST_Difference(polygon, group_area)" and its own Douglas-Peucker simplifier matching ST_Simplify's units. Migration `2026_08_25_000001_drop_rippling_reach_legacy_geometry` drops `rippling_reach.polygon` and its SPATIAL INDEX, `max_polygon` and `overflow_bounds` outright, keeping only the buffered `outer_bound`/`inner_bound` as an R-tree access path. Measured on the current tree: production ST_ set-operation calls (Union, Difference, Intersection, MakeValid, Buffer) number 29 in total, concentrated in ExpandService, ReachBoundsService, AuthorityStatsService and GroupBoundaryService. The other ~410 production ST_ sites across apiv2 and batch are ST_GeomFromText, ST_X/ST_Y, ST_AsText, ST_SRID, ST_Contains and ST_Envelope: parsing, formatting and point-in-polygon, all of which the sidecar already serves or trivially could. **A database with no spatial support is therefore no longer disqualified by that fact alone, and the same correction applies to the OceanBase and TiDB rejections, which both rest on it.** It also slightly discounts PostGIS as an argument for the core recommendation, though nothing else supporting that recommendation moves.
  - **The data model is the real reason.** CQL fixes the query pattern in the partition key at design time, has no joins, and has no general cross-partition transactions. Freegle joins constantly across users, memberships, groups, messages, chats and moderation state, and moderator and support work is ad-hoc cross-entity querying by definition. Moving spatial out of the database does nothing for any of that, so we would still need a relational system of record for everything else and Scylla would be an additional system beside it rather than a replacement: the opposite of what two people running this should want.
  - **The licence is the second reason, and reading the licence text rather than the launch coverage moves it up, not down.** The repository is public and actively developed (15.7k stars, commits on the day this was checked), so the source is open in the everyday sense, but `LICENSE-ScyllaDB-Source-Available.md` is the sole root licence and GitHub classifies it NOASSERTION rather than an OSI licence. AGPL 6.2 was the final open-source release and 6.2.x and earlier stay AGPL in perpetuity, but frozen at December 2024, so that escape hatch is not a usable system of record. Three clauses do the damage, in the order they would actually bite:
    1. **Modifying the software assigns the modification to ScyllaDB.** A modified version is a "Licensed Work" which "upon creation" is "the sole property of the Licensor", and the licensee "irrevocably and unconditionally assign[s] to the Licensor all the Licensed Work". This is not a contributor agreement triggered by submitting a patch: it triggers on creating one, whether or not it is ever sent upstream. Any local fix we carried would, by the text, be theirs.
    2. **"Never Customer" eligibility, with retroactive invoicing.** The grant exists only for entities with no paid subscription, professional services agreement or "any other commercial relationship" with ScyllaDB in the previous twelve months, as a strict condition precedent. For anyone else "no license is deemed to have been offered", any download is "unauthorized", use voids the licence *ab initio*, and ScyllaDB is entitled "to invoice Licensee for such unauthorized usage at Licensor's standard list prices, retroactive to the date of first use". Buying support once would retroactively unlicense the free production deployment across the whole organisation and its affiliates. Commercial customers get a carve-out for CI and test only.
    3. **The grant is revocable and the terms are unilaterally amendable.** "Changes will be effective upon posting to the Website or within the Software repository. Continued use of the Software after such changes constitutes acceptance."
  - Supporting terms, none decisive alone: a licence key may be imposed "as a condition of accessing or using the Software" with telemetry attached (the same concern that rejected CockroachDB in section 9); ScyllaDB may audit "Licensee's environment and corporate identity", and its records for a year after the term; liability is capped at $1,000 and explicitly excludes loss of data, for a system of record; governing law is Israel with exclusive jurisdiction in Tel Aviv.
  - **The terms have already moved once, which is the whole point.** Licence file history: AGPL added 2015-09-20; `treewide: relicense to ScyllaDB-Source-Available-1.0` on 2024-12-18; `LICENSE: Update to version 1.1` on 2026-04-12. That 1.1 revision is the amendment clause being exercised, and it added the entire Never Customer regime, the *ab initio* voiding, the retroactive invoicing and the corporate-identity audit right, sixteen months after the relicense, with continued use as the mechanism of acceptance. Our 28-vCPU, 137GB fleet sits inside the 10TB and/or 50 vCPU Usage Limit comfortably, so this was never about the cap. It is that a system of record would sit on a revocable grant that has already been tightened unilaterally once.

Foundations | Raw-SQL inventory script; PG18+PostGIS+Patroni dev cluster; team training starts; consultancy engaged; dbresolver-on-Postgres spike; decisions made up front: collation strategy (nondeterministic ICU vs citext per column, audit ILIKE), ENUM/SET handling, JSON-as-text vs jsonb | 3 pw |

Data migration + dual run | pgloader for plain tables (it does not auto-convert MySQL polygons, verified), bespoke WKB ETL with ST_MakeValid + SRID relabel for geometry tables; chunked parallel COPY for messages_likes (83M) and chat_messages (36M); **binlog CDC (pg_chameleon, or Debezium if we want heavier-duty) keeps Postgres caught up continuously**; port iznik-routing-go, and repoint iznik-spatial-go's six nightly rebuild source queries at Postgres (the sidecar itself, with its custom R-tree code, is deliberately unchanged; retiring it is out of scope); full connection-point inventory cutover list: apiv2, batch, routing-go, spatial-knn rebuild job, monitoring exporters, xtrabackup/backup scripts, the "yesterday" recovery system (rebuilt on pgBackRest), phpMyAdmin/support tools, monitor-fsm's DB access, CI (setup-test-database.sh + CircleCI orb run BOTH engines for the whole transition, a real ongoing CI cost) | 6 pw |
| 6. Shadow validation | Shadow-read harness live on hot paths (divergence to Loki), replay-and-diff for writes in staging, pgBackRest restore drill passed | 3 pw (2 to 4 weeks elapsed) |
| 7. Cutover | Go/no-go checklist (N clean dual-run days, divergence below threshold, restore drill passed, on-call staffed); short write-freeze in a low-traffic window (target under 15 minutes with CDC caught up; users see brief write failures or a read-only banner, reads unaffected); verify delta zero; flip env/dbresolver targets; smoke test. Time-boxed abort back to MySQL (never stopped) if verification fails | 1 pw |
| 8. Stabilise | Autovacuum/bloat/lag/pool monitoring; MySQL kept warm read-only for 2 to 4 weeks | 2 pw |

**Total ~37 to 43 person-weeks** including the contingency range on Phase 4 (4 to 8 calendar months depending on staffing).

**Rollback honesty:** rollback is free any time before cutover (MySQL stays authoritative; stopping CDC costs nothing). During the cutover window, the abort procedure is the rollback. Once real writes land on Postgres, rolling back means losing or hand-reconciling those writes (no reverse CDC is planned), so in practice the no-data-loss rollback expires at go-live plus zero; the 2 to 4 week warm-MySQL window is for reference and confidence, not free rollback. Say so out loud before cutover.

**If the port stalls partway:** a permanent split with some writes on MySQL and some on Postgres is not supportable (no cross-engine transactions, silent divergence risk). The only safe stalled state is Postgres remaining a read-only CDC-fed shadow indefinitely, with a hard decision point (suggest 3 months) to resource completion or formally shelve.

**If the Phase 1 PoC disappoints** (KNN/containment no better than our MBR-prefilter workaround on real data): the spatial justification weakens and the decision tree is: still proceed if the DDL pain alone justifies it (32% of changes needing manual runbooks says a lot), or stay on PXC 8.4 (optionally 2-node+garbd for the cost saving) and keep the ORM/portability work as standing code quality. That off-ramp is cheap because nothing engine-specific has been built yet at that point.

## 12. Risks and honest downsides

- Zero in-house Postgres production experience; autovacuum, WAL, Patroni and pgBackRest are genuinely new disciplines. Mitigation (training + external setup review + retainer) is budgeted but does not remove the 3am-incident gap while experience builds; train both people, staggered.
- The SRID relabel and ST_MakeValid cleanup can fail silently into "wrong nearby posts weeks later". Row-count parity does not prove geographic correctness; the PoC phase must include geometry-result parity checks, not just performance.
- Write-path verification is inherently weaker than read-path (no live shadowing); replay-and-diff in staging narrows but does not close that gap. This is where the residual risk concentrates, and why upserts get per-site integration tests on both dialects.
- ~37 to 43 person-weeks of opportunity cost against feature work, for a small team, with the usual risk of stall (mitigated by the read-only-shadow stalled state being safe).
- Async replication introduces a small failover data-loss window Galera did not have; remote_apply is opted into per path, not globally.
- Shadow testing adds read load to a currently saturated read node; fix db2's buffer pool first.
- CI runs two database engines for the duration; every branch pays that tax.
- The dual-run months need temporary extra infrastructure (~£150 to £250/mo) that the steady-state cost table excludes.

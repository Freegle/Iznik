# AI Intake Agent: Clarifying Conversations, Triage, and Routing — Design Patterns and Anti-Patterns

## Slot-filling / information-sufficiency: deciding what's missing and when to stop asking

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence base is old and specific to bug reports (Bettenburg et al. 2008/2010, Chaparro et al. 2017/2019), and it converges on a short, well-known checklist of fields (observed behaviour, expected behaviour, steps to reproduce, environment) that developers value highly but reporters rarely supply in full; newer LLM-agent research (2024-2026) shows models under-ask for clarification by default and that fixing this needs either training changes or an explicit uncertainty/information-gain model, not prompting alone.

### Cited Findings
- Bettenburg, Just, Schröter, Weiss, Premraj & Zimmermann, "What Makes a Good Bug Report?" (FSE 2008; journal version Zimmermann et al., IEEE TSE 2010) surveyed 466 developers/users of Apache, Eclipse and Mozilla and found a mismatch between what developers need and what users supply: steps to reproduce, stack traces and test cases are what developers find most helpful, but these are also the hardest things for users to provide — [What Makes a Good Bug Report? (ACM)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1453101.1453146), [IEEE TSE 2010 version](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSE.2010.63)
- The same team built CUEZILLA, a prototype that rates the quality of a new bug report and suggests specific improvements as it's being written — [What Makes a Good Bug Report? (PDF)](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/21fa/1dba8539598210496cfcdb853fef719a0805.pdf)
- Chaparro et al., "Detecting Missing Information in Bug Descriptions" (FSE 2017) manually analyzed ~3,000 bug reports: 93.5% report Observed Behaviour, but only 35.2% explicitly state Expected Behaviour and only 51.4% explicitly state Steps to Reproduce; reporters use 154 distinct discourse patterns to describe this content, which is why free-text parsing is hard — [Detecting Missing Information in Bug Descriptions](https://ojcchar.github.io/publications/8-fse17), DOI [10.1145/3106237.3106285](https://doi.org/10.1145/3106237.3106285)
- Chaparro et al., "Assessing the Quality of the Steps to Reproduce in Bug Reports" (ESEC/FSE 2019) introduces Euler, which classifies each reported step as Correct, Ambiguous, Vocabulary-Mismatched, or flags inferred **Missing Steps** between two given steps, and gives reporters feedback rather than just rejecting the report; one evaluator said the suggested missing steps "can guide the user to list them better," another that they "help avoid the guessing part when reproducing the bug" — [arXiv:1906.07107](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07107)
- BURT ("BUg ReporTing" chatbot, Song et al., ESEC/FSE 2022 + ICSE 2023 tool demo) runs a guided, task-oriented dialogue that walks an Android app end-user through supplying Observed Behaviour, Expected Behaviour and Steps to Reproduce, parses the free text with a dependency parser (Stanford CoreNLP, 16 sentence-type-specific parsing algorithms), checks each element's quality against a model of the app's actual UI/execution flow, and offers graphical suggestions as alternatives to typing more text; end-user evaluation on six Android apps found the guidance and automated suggestions useful and the tool easy to use — [BURT: A Chatbot for Interactive Bug Reporting (arXiv:2302.06050)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06050), [source (GitHub)](https://github.com/sea-lab-wm/burt)
- ClarifyGPT (Mu et al., FSE 2024, originally arXiv Oct 2023) frames clarification for *ambiguity detection*, not a fixed checklist: it samples several candidate solutions from an LLM, executes them against generated test inputs, and only triggers clarifying questions if the samples disagree (meaning the requirement really is ambiguous) — this deliberately avoids "unnecessary interactions... regarding well-defined requirements." On MBPP-sanitized it raised GPT-4 Pass@1 from 70.96% to 80.80% — [ClarifyGPT (arXiv:2310.10996)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10996)
- "Learning to Ask: When LLM Agents Meet Unclear Instruction" (arXiv 2409.00557, EMNLP 2025) built NoisyToolBench from real user queries/error patterns and proposes "Ask-when-Needed" (AwN): the model is prompted to weigh, at each tool-call step, whether it should ask for more information before invoking a tool; models that ask outperform models that guess — [arXiv:2409.00557](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.00557)
- "Modeling Future Conversation Turns to Teach LLMs to Ask Clarifying Questions" (Oct 2024, ICLR 2025) finds that current LLMs under-ask for clarification by default and attributes this to RLHF preference data being collected as single-turn rankings (annotators never see what a clarifying question would lead to); training on double-turn preferences (rewarding a clarifying question by what it leads to next) fixes the under-asking — [ICLR 2025 paper](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/97e2df4bb8b2f1913657344a693166a2-Paper-Conference.pdf)
- "Structured Uncertainty guided Clarification for LLM Agents" (arXiv 2511.08798) argues that without explicit modelling of parameter relationships, importance hierarchies and feasibility constraints, agents "lack principled criteria for determining which questions to ask and when to stop asking them" — i.e. a plain checklist isn't enough once fields interact — [arXiv:2511.08798](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.08798v2)
- CIGAsk (arXiv 2609.24290, recent) trains two coupled skills jointly — *when* to ask vs. answer, and *how* to phrase a question that actually recovers the disambiguating fact — using "counterfactual information gain" as the training signal, echoing older expected-information-gain approaches (Rao & Daumé III 2018 EVPI ranking; Aliannejadi et al. 2019/2020 conversational search) — [arXiv:2609.24290](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24290)

### Inferences
- For a bug-report-shaped intake schema, the field checklist itself is not the hard part — Bettenburg/Chaparro/BURT converge on OB / EB / S2R / environment — the hard part is (a) recognising that a submitted report is *missing* one of these fields from unstructured text (154+ discourse patterns per Chaparro 2017), and (b) not asking for fields that are already inferable from other signals (device/account from logs — see Key Q2/6).
- The ClarifyGPT "only ask if responses actually diverge" test (generate multiple candidate interpretations, ask only if they disagree) is a transferable stopping rule for a support-intake agent: draft a structured ticket, and only surface a clarifying question for a field where the draft is genuinely uncertain or contradictory, rather than asking against a static checklist regardless of context.
- The 2024-2026 clarifying-question literature is overwhelmingly about tool-calling/code-generation agents, not customer support intake specifically — the "ask vs. guess" tradeoff research transfers by analogy (asking beats guessing when guessing is wrong) but has not, as far as this research found, been validated on a support/bug-triage conversation dataset.

### Gaps
- No paper found that defines a per-category "required fields" schema specifically for a support/bug-intake LLM agent (as opposed to bug-tracker research or code-gen clarification) — this seems to be an implementation gap the report will need to fill by adapting Bettenburg/Chaparro's OB/EB/S2R model rather than citing an existing "support intake schema" paper.
- No controlled comparison found of "ask everything up front via a form/checklist" vs. "LLM-driven adaptive slot-filling" specifically for support tickets (as distinct from the bug-report-quality literature, which studies static forms/prose, not adaptive dialogue).

## Question volume and etiquette in async channels; batching vs. one-at-a-time; using existing context instead of asking

### Takeaway
Evidence here is thin and mostly anecdotal/vendor-sourced rather than peer-reviewed; the one repeatedly-stated principle across support-tooling vendors is to use context you already have (account data, logs) before asking the reporter, and to make any human handoff carry full context so the person isn't asked to re-supply what the agent already extracted — but no rigorous study of question-count-vs-drop-off in an LLM intake agent was found.
### Cited Findings
- A user review of a chat-style survey tool reports that showing "only one question at a time like a real conversation... drastically reduces drop-offs" versus traditional multi-field forms — this is a single customer testimonial, not a controlled study — [AWS Marketplace review](https://aws.amazon.com/marketplace/reviews/reviews-list/prodview-impaofvmoogne)
- Zendesk reports 85% of CX leaders say customers will abandon a brand over unresolved issues, even on first contact, underscoring the cost of friction/drop-off broadly (not specific to question count) — [Zendesk AI chatbot use cases](https://www.zendesk.com/blog/ai/chatbots/ai-chatbot-use-cases/)
- IrisAgent and other vendors recommend that a chatbot-to-human handoff carry full conversation context, since "customers forgive AI limits, not dead ends" — [IrisAgent](https://irisagent.com/ai-for-customer-support/)
- On the "reduce questions by using known context" side: OWASP's guidance and email-agent defense literature (see Key Q6) separately establish that inbound message metadata and account records are legitimately available context an agent can draw on before asking the reporter to repeat themselves — implied rather than stated as a stopping-question rule per se.
- Handoff-quality research (from Key Q7, Lorikeet 2026 and Bluetweak) states that a *good* human handoff includes "the full conversation transcript, detected customer intent, CRM data and account value, what the AI already tried, why it escalated" — i.e. the anti-pattern of re-asking a human what the agent already knows is treated as a measurable failure mode, with poorly-handed-off tickets taking 30-60 seconds longer per case and 30-50% longer to resolve — [Lorikeet: AI support resolution rate 2026](https://www.lorikeetcx.ai/articles/ai-support-resolution-rate-what-the-numbers-hide-2026), [Bluetweak: AI-to-human handoff 2026](https://www.bluetweak.com/blog/ai-to-human-handoff)

### Inferences
- Because no rigorous study of "how many clarifying questions before drop-off" exists, the safer design guidance to carry forward is procedural, drawn from adjacent research: (1) only ask about fields the draft ticket is actually uncertain about (ClarifyGPT's "ask only if ambiguous," Key Q1); (2) prefer forced-choice / multiple-choice questions over open free text where possible, since this reduces reporter effort and reduces the "154 discourse patterns" parsing burden (Chaparro 2017) that comes from unconstrained prose; (3) batch all currently-known-missing questions into a single message per turn in an async channel (email/forum) rather than a back-and-forth per field, since round-trip latency in email/forum is measured in hours-to-days, not seconds — this is a reasonable inference from channel latency, not a cited finding.
- The absence of hard data specifically on "chatbot question count vs abandonment" for AI intake agents (as opposed to lead-gen or CSAT surveys) should be flagged to the report writer as a genuine research gap, not glossed over with the form-based UX literature, which is a different context (synchronous web forms) from asynchronous email/forum triage.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on optimal number of clarifying questions per turn, specifically for LLM support/bug-intake agents operating over asynchronous channels (email/forum), nor on synchronous vs. asynchronous drop-off comparisons for this use case.
- No study found that isolates "batch all questions in one message" vs "ask one question, wait, ask the next" for reporter completion rates in a real support-intake deployment; the search surfaced only a general web-form completion testimonial and general CX abandonment statistics, both non-specific to LLM agents.

## Urgency/severity/intent classification with LLMs: reliability, calibration, rubrics, and combining with monitoring signals

### Takeaway
LLM severity/urgency classifiers are usable in production but only when anchored by a written rubric with worked examples and a decision procedure (which measurably raises inter-rater agreement) and paired with confidence-based routing to humans; unaided LLM judgment shows poor agreement even between versions of the same model family.
### Cited Findings
- incident.io (2026) describes severity models classifying alerts from payload content, the affected service's catalog criticality, and patterns from past incidents, and explicitly recommends confidence thresholds that route unfamiliar alerts to humans because "a confident wrong answer is worse than no answer"; it also notes that AI triage "inherits every blind spot in your monitoring" — a model can be very confident over the visible slice of the system while failures propagate through unmonitored parts — [incident.io: AI incident triage](https://incident.io/blog/ai-incident-triage)
- The same source recommends building a test set from your own past incidents with known ground truth (actual severity, actual root cause, actual owning team) and measuring the model's match rate against it before trusting it — [incident.io: AI incident triage](https://incident.io/blog/ai-incident-triage)
- "Incident Copilots" (Zenodo, May 2026) proposes a concrete evaluation plan: an A/B test on N≥200 historical incidents measuring MTTA (mean time to acknowledge), TTFC (time to first classification/comment), handoff latency, MTTR (median and p90), a 0-10 document-completeness rubric, targeting recall@k ≥ 0.80 and citation coverage ≥ 0.90, plus human verification of 50-100 AI-produced documents per month specifically to bound hallucination — [Incident Copilots (Zenodo)](https://zenodo.org/records/20157213)
- An arXiv study on LLM-graded clinical notes found that two successive generations of the *same* model family, grading the same 171 matched cases independently without a rubric, agreed at Cohen's kappa 0.177 (near-chance), with a systematic directional bias (of 52 cases graded "supporting" by one generation, the other graded 37 of them "critical"). After the authors wrote a rubric with three grade definitions, worked anchors, and a four-step decision procedure, agreement between two *different* model families rose to kappa 0.662 [95% CI 0.59-0.73] — nearly 4x higher than the unanchored same-family pair. A side effect: both rubric-anchored graders scored more severely than either model did unaided — [LLM Judges Verify Presence, Not Absence (arXiv:2608.31016)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.31016)
- A Neural Base course on LLM red-teaming severity classification recommends borrowing the CVSS/OWASP-style likelihood × impact grid (likelihood: rare/occasional/frequent; impact: annoying/serious/catastrophic) as a repeatable severity rubric structure, arguing this gives "a repeatable way to measure real-world impact, not just theoretical capability" — [The Neural Base: severity classification](https://theneuralbase.com/red-teaming-llms/learn/advanced/severity-classification/)
- Weights & Biases' LLM-evaluation guide states a safety/severity rubric needs the protected category, severity, allowed transformations, and an escalation path spelled out, and warns that "automated classifiers are useful for triage, but they can miss context, coded language, or multi-step harm" — [W&B: LLM evaluation](https://wandb.ai/onlineinference/genai-research/reports/LLM-evaluation-Metrics-frameworks-and-best-practices--VmlldzoxMTMxNjQ4NA)
- Clinical-triage-adjacent work found calibration is heterogeneous by class — one urgency class was systematically overconfident with sparse probability coverage in the middle range — underscoring that "the model is X% confident" cannot be trusted uniformly across severity tiers without per-class calibration checks — [arXiv:2511.17124 context / general clinical triage calibration finding cited via search synthesis]

### Inferences
- The clinical-notes kappa result (0.177 → 0.662 via rubric) is the single strongest piece of *quantitative* evidence in this whole research pass for a general design principle: **any LLM classification step (severity, urgency, category, duplicate/not-duplicate) needs a written rubric with worked examples and a decision procedure, not just a category name in the prompt**, because unaided agreement can be barely above chance even between checkpoints of one model.
- Combining LLM severity output with monitoring signals (error rates, affected-user counts from Sentry/Loki-style systems) is implied by the incident.io material (payload + catalog criticality + historical pattern) but not measured with a specific accuracy delta in any source found; treat "combining with monitoring signals improves accuracy" as a reasonable inference from how production systems are described, not as a cited statistic.

### Gaps
- No source quantified how much adding monitoring-signal features (e.g. number of distinct reporters, Sentry event count, affected-user count) improves severity-classification accuracy over LLM-text-only classification — this would need to be measured against Freegle's own historical ticket data rather than cited from literature.
- No source directly benchmarked LLM intent classification (as opposed to severity) reliability/calibration for a support-intake context specifically.

## Deduplication and clustering into underlying problems; ITIL incident vs. problem

### Takeaway
Production and open-source dedup systems converge on a two-stage pipeline — cheap embedding-similarity search for candidates, then an LLM (or human) judgment only on the ambiguous middle band — with thresholds always tuned against a labelled set rather than guessed; ITIL's incident/problem split maps directly onto "report" (incident: the visible symptom, restore fast) vs. "underlying bug" (problem: root cause, fix once).
### Cited Findings
- Linear's "Similar Issues" (Triage Intelligence) feature uses LLMs and vector embeddings together, explicitly aimed at "the challenge of managing duplicate issues and backlog in large team workflows"; for scale, Linear partitions its embeddings table by workspace ID across several hundred partitions with per-partition indexes — [Linear: Using AI to detect similar issues](https://linear.app/now/using-ai-to-detect-similar-issues)
- Linear's fuller engineering write-up, "How we built Triage Intelligence" (Sept 2025), states the product combines search, ranking and LLM-based reasoning over the existing backlog, surfaces duplicate/related-issue suggestions and label/assignee recommendations with a visible explanation, and was deliberately built around trust and transparency ("if you are going to act on AI-generated suggestions, you need to see where they came from"); processing a new issue is expected to take **1-4 minutes** for high-quality suggestions, because the underlying reasoning/matching is slower than Linear's usual deterministic systems though still fast next to a human — [Linear: How we built Triage Intelligence](https://linear.app/now/how-we-built-triage-intelligence)
- A self-hosted duplicate detector (IssueSense) computes cosine similarity in Postgres via pgvector and *deliberately does not act on its own* — it only surfaces a suggestion (API response or comment) and "leaves the actual 'is this really a duplicate' call to a person," never auto-closing — [Asif-Nawaz27/github_issue_duplicate_detector](https://github.com/Asif-Nawaz27/github_issue_duplicate_detector)
- A worked threshold-tuning example (buma project, fastembed BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5, 384-dim): a naive 0.9 cosine-similarity threshold was found too strict — spot checks showed obvious duplicates scoring 0.81-0.87, one true-duplicate pair scored 0.869, and an unrelated pair scored ~0.49 — so the team held off enabling automatic comments until the threshold was set from a labelled precision/recall evaluation rather than a round number — [Zeeldesai12345/buma PR #4](https://github.com/Zeeldesai12345/buma/pull/4)
- A GitHub Action (mackgorski/ai-duplicate-detector) auto-labels/closes issues above a configured threshold and gives explicit tuning guidance: "if nothing is detected, the thresholds might be set too high; if there are too many false positives, increase the threshold values" — an example of full automation (as opposed to IssueSense's suggest-only stance) — [ai-duplicate-detector](https://github.com/marketplace/actions/ai-powered-github-issue-duplicates-relations-detector)
- The general pattern recommended in a spec for entity-resolution deduplication: use vector similarity for the bulk of cases, and reserve LLM-based verification only for the ambiguous band between the auto-merge and auto-reject thresholds — a way of using an expensive judge sparingly — [aLayaSiki issue #68](https://github.com/takurot/aLayaSiki/issues/68)
- ITIL definitions: an **incident** is "an unplanned disruption to service or the failure of a service component"; a **problem** is "a cause or potential cause of one or more incidents," often not immediately visible to end users but capable of causing recurring or more serious incidents if unresolved. Incident management prioritises speed of restoration even without a known root cause; problem management performs root-cause analysis and may formally document a **known error** with a workaround while a permanent fix is pursued. ITIL further splits problem management into *reactive* (after incidents occur) and *proactive* (looking for problems before they cause incidents) — [Atlassian: Problem vs incident management](https://www.atlassian.com/incident-management/devops/incident-vs-problem-management), [IBM: Incident vs problem management](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/incident-management-vs-problem-management), [ITIL.org.uk](https://www.itil.org.uk/blog/incident-management-vs-problem-management)

### Inferences
- The suggest-only vs. auto-close split (IssueSense vs. mackgorski's action) is a genuine design fork with different risk profiles: suggest-only is safe by construction but adds human review load; auto-close/auto-label needs a labelled eval set to set thresholds responsibly (as buma's team did) or it will misfire in both directions. Given this project already has a "never auto-close on the reporter's behalf without evidence" instinct (per project conventions), the suggest-only pattern with a labelled-threshold LLM-verification middle band is the safer starting point.
- Mapping ITIL onto an AI intake agent: each individual report is an **incident** in ITIL terms — get *that reporter* an actionable path (workaround, explanation, or handoff) quickly. Clustering multiple incidents that share a cause is the **problem** — and per ITIL's own framing, problem identification is "triggered by repeating incidents, patterns, or trend analysis," i.e. clustering/dedup output (Key Q4's embedding pipeline) is precisely the trigger for opening a "problem" record distinct from each individual ticket. This suggests the agent's data model should have two tiers: report/incident (1:1 with a reporter's conversation) and a separate problem/cluster entity that many incidents link to — consistent with how Sentry issues (many events → one issue) and GitHub "duplicate of #X" already work.

### Gaps
- No source found that explicitly discusses linking a support-intake conversation to an *existing Sentry issue* as the dedup target (as distinct from GitHub-issue-to-GitHub-issue dedup, which was well covered). This is a plausible but uncited design choice for a system already using Sentry, per the project's own monitor-fsm workflow.
- No large-scale accuracy numbers (precision/recall) were found for the LLM-judge-on-ambiguous-band approach; only threshold anecdotes (0.81-0.87 range) from one small project.

## State machine / async conversation workflow: waiting timers, reminders, auto-close, reopening, handoff, approval gates, audit trail

### Takeaway
No single authoritative reference model was found; the pattern is assembled piecemeal from helpdesk-platform behaviour and general agent-governance guidance, and converges on: a "waiting on reporter" state that pauses SLA clocks and is exited by any reply, one or more time-boxed reminders with a state field to prevent duplicate sends, an eventual auto-close with an explicit reopen-on-reply path, and — separately — every state transition and any agent-initiated external action logged to an append-only audit trail with human approval gates scaled to the action's risk/reversibility.
### Cited Findings
- Intercom's ticket engine natively reopens a "Waiting on customer" ticket back to "In progress" the moment the customer replies, and pauses/un-pauses the SLA clock across that transition; but a reply to an already-*Resolved* ticket does **not** auto-reopen — that requires an explicit workflow rule — [Intercom: How ticket states work](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/9730130-how-ticket-states-work)
- Freshdesk community guidance for reminder timing uses a time-window condition (e.g. "between 48 and 50 hours") plus a custom flag field so the reminder can't fire more than once per ticket — [Freshworks community thread](https://community.freshworks.com/automations-and-workflows-11361/automation-reminder-to-the-customer-that-the-agent-are-waiting-on-a-reply-46129)
- A HubSpot community thread notes that a workflow must have re-enrollment explicitly enabled on "last customer reply date updated" for the ticket to be able to flip state back and forth more than once — a good machine-state gotcha (the naive workflow only fires once ever) — [HubSpot community](https://community.hubspot.com/t5/Tickets-Conversations/Ticket-status-to-be-changed-to-Client-followup-when-ticket-is/m-p/1247334)
- Zoho Desk community discussion lays out the full desired pattern explicitly: daily reminders for X days while "awaiting their response," then a distinct final message explaining the ticket will be closed but can be reopened by replying — motivated partly by workload/queue hygiene ("leaving these tickets in the open view clutters the agent's queue... makes it difficult to load balance tickets") — [Zoho community](https://help.zoho.com/portal/nl/community/topic/automate-reminder-emails-for-tickets-with-status-waiting-for-customer-and-then-auto-close-after-x-days)
- On the GitHub-issue side, Jacob Tomlinson's widely-cited (Dec 2024) argument: a stale/auto-close bot should **only** ever act on issues where the label indicates the *reporter* is the party who needs to act (e.g. "needs info," "needs reproducible example") — never on issues waiting on a maintainer — because auto-closing something the maintainer owes a response on punishes the wrong party — [Jacob Tomlinson: Most stale bots are anti-user](https://jacobtomlinson.dev/posts/2024/most-stale-bots-are-anti-user-and-anti-contributor-but-they-dont-have-to-be/)
- Dosu's better-stale-bot (May 2026) implements an AI-read-before-acting version: default policy is 60 days before labeling stale, 7 more days before closing, and if a non-bot user replies at any point, the Stale label is removed rather than the countdown continuing blindly — [Dosu: an AI stale bot that reads first](https://dosu.dev/blog/an-ai-stale-bot-that-you-can-trust)
- A concrete production example (AWS Powertools for Lambda/TypeScript) uses a dedicated `need-response` label reserved specifically for triggering stale automation (distinct from other labels), moving through `status/pending-close-response-required` after 2 weeks of no reply and to `status/rejected` after one more week — a clean illustration of using a purpose-built label rather than overloading an existing "needs info" label with automation semantics — [AWS Powertools PR #1390](https://github.com/aws-powertools/powertools-lambda-typescript/pull/1390)
- On approval gates: a widely-repeated three-tier risk model — auto-approve for safe/reversible actions, a "notify" soft gate (agent proceeds, human notified, can undo) for recoverable-but-impactful actions, and a hard block requiring explicit prior approval for irreversible/high-stakes actions (delete data, send money, publish content, execute side-effecting code) — [Hitchhiker's Guide to Agentic AI (arXiv:2606.24937)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.24937)
- Tech Arion's governance framing: gate on **irreversibility and blast radius**, not on the agent's own confidence, because "a confident agent acting on a poisoned instruction is exactly the case oversight exists to catch" — [Tech Arion: AI agent governance](https://techarion.com/blog/ai-agent-governance-audit-trails-compliance)
- Audit-trail requirement, stated plainly: "If you can't show who approved what, when, and why, you don't have a defensible approval workflow" — recommended as append-only logging with a defined retention policy, searchable during incidents/audits — [StackAI: Human-in-the-loop AI agents](https://www.stackai.com/insights/human-in-the-loop-ai-agents-how-to-design-approval-workflows-for-safe-and-scalable-automation)
- Named failure modes to design against: **rubber-stamp approvals** (reviewers start clicking approve without reading, "automation bias"); **gating too late** ("if you gate after the side effect, it's not approval, it's incident documentation"); and **approving blind** (an approval request with no evidence pack turns a 15-second decision into a 15-minute investigation) — [DEV Community: HITL AI agents](https://dev.to/rish_poddar/human-in-the-loop-ai-agents-approvals-permissions-and-audit-trails-i0f)

### Inferences
- Putting the helpdesk-platform patterns and the GitHub-stale-bot patterns together gives a coherent state machine for an AI intake agent: `New → Awaiting Reporter (question sent, SLA paused) → [any reply] → Triaging → Actionable (handed to human/fixer) → Resolved → [reply within window reopens] Reopened`, with a parallel reminder sub-state (`Awaiting Reporter: reminder 1 sent`, `reminder 2 sent`, ...) guarded by a flag to prevent duplicate sends, and an explicit final "closing, but reply reopens" message before auto-close — mirroring Zoho's requested pattern and Intercom's native reopen-on-reply behaviour.
- Tomlinson's principle (never auto-close/auto-expire something waiting on the *maintainer*/human side) generalises directly: the state machine must distinguish "waiting on reporter" from "waiting on human triager," and only the former is eligible for reminder/auto-close automation. This is an important anti-pattern to flag: a poorly-built "waiting" state that doesn't distinguish who owes the next reply will incorrectly auto-close tickets that are actually stuck on the *team's* side.
- The approval-gate risk tiers map naturally onto intake-agent actions: auto-approve (asking a clarifying question, drafting a structured ticket), soft-gate-with-notify (sending an automated reminder, applying a label), hard-block (closing a ticket, replying with account-specific data, escalating/de-escalating severity that changes an SLA, any action that emails a large set of members) — this mapping is this document's synthesis, not a cited claim.

### Gaps
- No source specifically describes a *reopening* window policy (e.g. "replies within 30 days reopen; after that, a new ticket") tuned for a volunteer-run/charity support context; the helpdesk examples found are generic SaaS/enterprise patterns.
- No source found benchmarking optimal reminder cadence/count (e.g. 1 reminder vs. 2) against reporter response likelihood — the Zoho/Freshdesk material describes *how* to build reminders, not evidence on how many are effective before diminishing returns or annoyance.

## Safety: prompt injection, PII/data leakage, GDPR, and identity verification

### Takeaway
Inbound email/forum text must be treated as fully untrusted (indirect prompt injection is OWASP's #1 LLM risk in both 2025 and 2026 editions, and the "lethal trifecta" of private data + untrusted content + external communication is exactly the shape of a mail-reading support agent); the consensus defense is structural (limit what the agent can *do*, gate sends on human approval, restrict who can reach its inbox) rather than relying on prompt-level filtering, and GDPR compliance for an intake agent centres on minimal data collection, a defined retention/deletion policy, and identity verification before surfacing account-specific data.
### Cited Findings
- OWASP ranks Prompt Injection as **LLM01**, its top LLM risk, in both the 2025 and 2026 editions — [OWASP GenAI: LLM01 Prompt Injection](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm01-prompt-injection/)
- OWASP's own example is a support chatbot specifically: "An attacker injects a prompt into a customer support chatbot, instructing it to ignore previous guidelines, query private data stores, and send emails, leading to unauthorized access and privilege escalation," and cites a real CVE (CVE-2024-5184) against an LLM-powered email assistant exploited to access sensitive information and manipulate email content — [OWASP GenAI: LLM01](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm01-prompt-injection/)
- Simon Willison's "lethal trifecta" concept — private data + untrusted content + external communication capability, all present at once — is cited as the reason email agents are structurally dangerous: inbound messages are the untrusted content, the mailbox is the private data, the send capability is the external-communication vector — [via arXiv 2507.02699 discussion](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02699v2)
- A 2026 academic paper (ESPI, arXiv:2507.02699) devised email-specific prompt injections with a 73.54% overall attack success rate against real-world LLM email agents, substantially beating older baseline attacks; the disclosure produced 16 assigned CVEs across 22 vendors that formally acknowledged the risk. The same paper found that older, generic injection phrasings ("ignore the previous instructions") now consistently fail against premium models — real, tailored attacks are what succeed — [Your Mailbox Is Mine (arXiv:2507.02699)](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02699v2)
- Immersive Labs demonstrated an indirect prompt injection hidden in an email's HTML that manipulates an AI assistant into reconstructing and presenting a malicious link to the end user — bypassing traditional signature/reputation-based email-security scanning entirely, because the compromise happens at the AI-interaction stage, not the mail-delivery stage — [Immersive Labs: Weaponizing LLMs](https://www.immersivelabs.com/resources/c7-blog/weaponizing-llms-bypassing-email-security-products-via-indirect-prompt-injection)
- Nylas's defense guidance, aimed at exactly this problem: treat all email/calendar content as untrusted input the agent must never treat as instructions; restrict what the agent is *able* to do (so even a fully compromised model can't reach an unlisted recipient — "containment does not depend on the agent making the right decision"); require human approval before any high-risk/sensitive action (e.g., sending mail, deleting data); layer defenses (separate metadata from untrusted bodies, sandbox by policy, human-approve sends, log every action); and — where feasible — filter senders before mail arrives, since an allowlist at the SMTP level makes injection from unknown senders "structurally impossible," because "there's nothing to sanitize... or accidentally obey" for rejected mail. Nylas also suggests giving the agent its own dedicated inbox address rather than connecting it to a person's mailbox — [Nylas: Email prompt injection defense](https://cli.nylas.com/guides/email-prompt-injection-defense)
- Caveat for a public-facing support agent specifically: it "can't allowlist its customers," so for that inbound path the permission limits and send-approval gate must do essentially all of the protective work (sender allowlisting doesn't apply) — synthesis drawn from the same source set.
- On GDPR: any chatbot/agent handling personal data of EU (and by extension UK GDPR / UK residents, given equivalent domestic law) residents is in scope regardless of vendor location; a DPA is required under Art. 28 where a third-party processor is used; users retain rights to be informed (Art. 13), access (Art. 15) and erasure (Art. 17) — [heyData: GDPR-compliant AI agents](https://heydata.eu/en/magazine/how-to-make-ai-agents-gdpr-compliant)
- One source surveyed made an explicit legal error worth flagging: it claimed GDPR "requires explicit consent for all processing" — this is false; GDPR Article 6 lists six lawful bases for processing (consent is only one; contract performance and legitimate interests are others) — noted here so the report doesn't propagate the error — [context via arXiv:2503.18387 discussion, corrected against GDPR Art. 6 text]
- Identity verification pattern for signed-in users: JWT-based identity verification lets an agent recognise an authenticated customer while keeping sensitive token contents out of the model's own context — the verified identity, not raw token secrets, is what's used for account-aware actions — [Chatbase docs, referenced via search synthesis]
- Minimal-data-collection guidance: "a customer service chatbot should stick to asking for order numbers and basic verification details, avoiding unnecessary personal information" — [Crescendo: AI and GDPR 2026](https://www.crescendo.ai/blog/ai-and-gdpr)
- A concrete retention example: if conversations are stored for training/analysis, auto-delete "after 60 days, for instance — unless users have consented to longer storage" — [heyData: GDPR-compliant AI agents](https://heydata.eu/en/magazine/how-to-make-ai-agents-gdpr-compliant)

### Inferences
- For a charity support-intake agent reading inbound email/forum threads from the public, the sender-allowlist defense (Nylas) is not available — anyone can email in — so the load-bearing controls have to be (a) tight limits on what the agent's tools can *do* regardless of what the message says (no send-as-admin, no bulk email, no DB writes without a gate), and (b) mandatory human approval before any action that touches another member's personal data or sends outbound communication at scale. This directly supports keeping "reply to reporter" and "escalate/create ticket" as low-risk auto-approved actions, while "look up another user's account" or "send a mass notification" sit behind a hard approval gate.
- Because reports can be *about* other members (e.g. "so-and-so isn't collecting the item"), the agent's inbound-untrusted-content problem compounds with a not-leaking-third-party-PII problem: any tool call that would surface another member's contact details or account history in a reply to the *original* reporter is a second, GDPR-flavoured injection risk distinct from the classic "send my emails to attacker@evil.com" pattern, and needs its own explicit block regardless of what the inbound message asks for.

### Gaps
- No source specifically addresses UK GDPR / Data Protection Act 2018 nuances for a UK charity (as opposed to generic "GDPR" vendor content, which conflates EU and UK regimes); Freegle-specific data-protection posture (e.g. its existing privacy policy, ICO registration) was not looked up in this pass and should be checked internally rather than from these general sources.
- No source found on identity verification specifically for *unauthenticated* channels (an email or forum post from someone not logged in) — the JWT pattern found assumes a signed-in web/app session, which doesn't fit a purely email/forum-based report.

## Evaluation: metrics and replay/eval sets

### Takeaway
Industry practice (mostly vendor-published, treat benchmarks cautiously) has converged on tracking automated-resolution-rate and handoff-rate together with CSAT and a 24-72h repeat-contact rate, because any one of these numbers alone can be gamed or misread; separately, engineering teams increasingly build offline eval datasets from anonymized historical tickets/traces with frozen retrieved context so replays are deterministic, and score each expected behaviour separately rather than one pass/fail per conversation.
### Cited Findings
- Vendors report having "largely converged" on a common resolution-rate definition: resolved without human handoff **and** the customer doesn't recontact within 72 hours — making the metric secretly two metrics glued together (resolution + non-recurrence) — [Lorikeet: AI support resolution rate, what the numbers hide (2026)](https://www.lorikeetcx.ai/articles/ai-support-resolution-rate-what-the-numbers-hide-2026)
- Handoff rate is explicitly warned against reading as "lower is better": Lorikeet states a healthy handoff rate sits **15-30%** depending on complexity, and a reported 3% handoff rate in a regulated queue "is not outperforming the field. It is failing to escalate" — a rate that low can also come from never disclosing to the user that they're talking to an AI and never offering an easy route to a human, so "resolved" quietly means "the customer gave up" — [Lorikeet](https://www.lorikeetcx.ai/articles/ai-support-resolution-rate-what-the-numbers-hide-2026)
- Resolution rate must be read as difficulty-adjusted: one industry tiering puts basic FAQ chatbots at 20-40% resolution, standard assistants with embedded business logic at 40-60%, and "true agentic platforms" at 70-85%; a 2026 statistics roundup cites median tier-1 deflection of 41.2% across enterprise CX in 2026 (top quartile 58.7%), with refund/password-reset intents deflecting 70%+ while "nuanced complaints rarely break 25%" — [Notch: AI customer service metrics 2026](https://www.notch.cx/post/customer-service-ai-metrics), [Digital Applied: customer service AI agent statistics 2026](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/customer-service-ai-agent-statistics-2026-data)
- Handoff-quality economics: poorly-handed-off tickets (context dumped as a bare transcript) cost agents an extra 30-60 seconds re-reading, and resolve 30-50% slower overall than tickets that went straight to a human, with financial-services handoffs degrading 40-60% and technical-support handoffs 20-35% — a good handoff instead includes "the full conversation transcript, detected customer intent, CRM data and account value, what the AI already tried, why it escalated, and a suggested response" — [Lorikeet](https://www.lorikeetcx.ai/articles/ai-support-resolution-rate-what-the-numbers-hide-2026), [Bluetweak: AI-to-human handoff 2026](https://www.bluetweak.com/blog/ai-to-human-handoff)
- Recommended weekly scorecard from one vendor: resolution rate, CSAT on AI-handled tickets, CSAT on escalated tickets, and agent handle time on escalations — tracked as four numbers together rather than any single headline figure — [Fini Labs: AI support platforms 2026](https://www.usefini.com/guides/ai-support-platforms-human-agent-handoff)
- Named failure signature: "low CSAT with high [automated resolution rate] may reveal forced closures or unresolved automation loops" — i.e. a high resolution number paired with low satisfaction is itself diagnostic of a bad agent, not a good one — [Zendesk: AI service quality metrics](https://www.zendesk.com/blog/ai/workflow-automation/ai-service-quality-metrics/)
- On replay/offline eval construction: a customer-service-agent paper curated 605 anonymized historical dialogue sessions / 1,427 turns from production logs, then cleaned, deduplicated and filtered to an FAQ subset of 581 turn-level queries split 492/52/37 across train/dev/held-out test — a concrete, citable example of the scale and split ratios used to build a support-agent eval set from real tickets — [Evidence-in-the-Loop (arXiv:2607.18039)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.18039)
- General agent-eval survey: offline eval (pre-generated static datasets) is "comparatively cheaper and simpler to run and maintain" but "typically lack[s] the nuance to fully address the wide range of responses an LLM agent may be able or expected to provide" — implying offline eval should gate changes, while online/production monitoring finds what the dataset is missing — [Evaluation and Benchmarking of LLM Agents: A Survey (arXiv:2507.21504)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.21504)
- Practical replay-fidelity tip: pre-compute retrieval results offline and bake the exact chunks into each eval-dataset row, so a replay receives the same context the model would have seen live, rather than re-running retrieval (which could silently drift) at eval time — [LaunchDarkly: Offline evaluation of RAG-grounded answers](https://launchdarkly.com/docs/tutorials/offline-evals)
- Eval-set staleness warning: "if you freeze an eval set for a quarter, the set ages off the product within weeks" — the recommended fix is to promote newly-observed failing production traces into the offline eval set on a weekly cadence rather than treating the eval set as static — [FutureAGI: LLM agent evaluation guide 2026](https://futureagi.com/blog/llm-agent-evaluation-complete-guide-2026/)
- Scoring granularity: score each expected behaviour of a support agent separately (e.g., did it follow the refund/escalation policy; did it actually use a tool's result in its final answer; did it avoid unnecessary steps) rather than a single pass/fail per conversation, since a single aggregate score hides which specific behaviour regressed — [Arize: LLM evaluation guide](https://arize.com/resources/llm-evaluation/)
- Privacy note for building a shareable eval set: raw production traces contain private user data and generally "cannot be released directly"; instead publish sanitized fixtures (planner outputs, candidate actions, prompt templates, judge outputs) rather than raw transcripts if the eval set will be shared beyond the immediate team — [Self-GC (arXiv:2607.00692)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.00692)

### Inferences
- The Lorikeet "3% handoff rate in a regulated queue is a failure to escalate, not a success" framing is directly transferable as an anti-pattern warning: for a support-intake agent it would be tempting to report a very high "handled without human" rate as a success metric, but per this cited pattern that number needs to always be read together with reporter satisfaction and 72-hour repeat-contact rate, or it risks rewarding the agent for quietly giving up on hard cases rather than escalating them.
- "Time-to-actionable" (one of the metrics named in the task's key questions) wasn't directly named in any source found, but maps closely onto the incident-response metrics that *were* found (MTTA/TTFC from Incident Copilots) — the closest cited analogue is time-to-first-classification/comment, which is conceptually the same measurement applied to "how long until this report has enough structured information to route," just not phrased that way in support-agent literature.

### Gaps
- No source directly reports "questions asked per ticket" as a tracked metric anywhere in the vendor or academic literature surveyed — this appears to be a metric the report/design should introduce rather than one already established in the field.
- No source gave a specific "human override rate" definition or benchmark for an AI *intake/triage* agent specifically (as distinct from a resolution-agent's handoff rate, which is a related but not identical concept — override implies a human disagreeing with/correcting a triage decision the agent already made, not just taking over an unresolved case).

## Real-world write-ups (2024-2026): GitHub, Linear, Sentry, Dosu, and "needs more info" bots

### Takeaway
The clearest, most detailed engineering write-up found is Linear's own "How we built Triage Intelligence" (Sept 2025); Sentry's Seer/Seer Agent line and its Linear integration show a mature root-cause-analysis-to-fix-PR pipeline with human approval retained at the "mark done" step; Dosu's 2026 pivot (ending free hosted triage, open-sourcing its labeling/stale-bot pieces, partnering with Pullfrog) is a live cautionary data point about the commercial fragility of hosted OSS-triage-bot offerings; and one independent team's published cost/benefit teardown (Cotera) found their in-house triage bot wasn't worth keeping despite working.
### Cited Findings
- **Linear — Triage Intelligence** (Sept 2025 engineering post): combines search, ranking, and LLM reasoning over the existing backlog to flag duplicates, link related issues, and recommend labels/assignees, each suggestion with a visible explanation; built around trust and transparency as explicit design goals; processing is expected to take 1-4 minutes per new issue, an explicit, stated trade-off of thoroughness over the sub-second latency of Linear's normal deterministic features — [Linear: How we built Triage Intelligence](https://linear.app/now/how-we-built-triage-intelligence)
- Linear's Customer Experience team post describes **triage rules**: actions that run automatically the moment Triage Intelligence finishes analysing an issue, i.e. a declarative policy layer sitting on top of the ML suggestions rather than the ML output acting directly — [Linear: How our CX team works in Linear](https://linear.app/now/cx-in-linear)
- **Sentry — Seer / Seer Agent**: Root Cause Analysis automatically scans incoming issues and feeds additional context into alerts/automated triage; Sentry describes a real example where an engineer handed Seer Agent only a raw issue, and the agent independently pulled the trace, cross-referenced it against other model/provider calls, and surfaced that one specific model family was failing in specific regions while others were fine — an autonomous root-cause narrative rather than a canned classification — [Sentry: Introducing Seer Agent](https://blog.sentry.io/introducing-seer-agent/)
- **Sentry-Linear integration**: Linear triage rules can auto-assign the Sentry agent to any newly created Linear issue synced from Sentry, making detection-to-root-cause-to-suggested-fix "hands-free"; marking the Linear issue Done resolves the linked Sentry issue automatically via bidirectional sync — human judgment is retained precisely at the "is this actually done" gate, while everything upstream of it (fetch context, propose fix) is automated — [Sentry: Auto-triage bugs with the Linear integration](https://sentry.io/cookbook/fix-bugs-sentry-agent-linear/), [docs.sentry.io: Sentry-Linear agent](https://docs.sentry.io/organization/integrations/issue-tracking/sentry-linear-agent/)
- **Sentry's own internal usage**: a Claude-Routines-plus-Sentry-MCP pipeline automatically triaged 800 AI-agent conversations overnight and filed bugs from them; one run found ~21% of sampled conversations (83/400) hit at least one tool error, mostly self-correcting retries — a rare disclosed example of an org using an LLM triage pipeline against its *own* product's agent-conversation logs, not just external bug reports — [Sentry: Automate daily AI agent error triage with Claude Routines](https://sentry.io/cookbook/automate-ai-agent-triage-claude-routines/), [blog: Claude Routines agent triage](https://blog.sentry.io/claude-routines-agent-triage/)
- Sentry's stated data-privacy position: it does not use customer error/source-code data to train generative models, and AI output derived from a customer's data is shown only to that customer — [docs.sentry.io/product/ai-in-sentry/seer](https://docs.sentry.io/product/ai-in-sentry/seer)
- **Dosu's 2026 pivot**: an August 2026 blog post announced the end of Dosu's unlimited free tier for open source (affecting the top ~4% of high-volume projects; ~96% see no change; CNCF projects keep elevated limits); going forward, hosted issue-triage work is being handed to a partner, Pullfrog, while Dosu open-sourced its own auto-labeling and stale-issue tools (`auto-label`, `better-stale-bot`) so projects can run them themselves in GitHub Actions for the cost of Actions minutes plus model usage — a concrete illustration that a hosted third-party triage bot is not a permanently stable dependency to build on — [Dosu: OSS has changed. So has Dosu](https://dosu.dev/blog/oss-has-changed-so-has-dosu)
- **Pullfrog** (built by Colin McDonnell, creator of Zod): an open-source, model-agnostic, BYOK bot that runs entirely inside GitHub Actions, doing PR review, issue triage, and coding tasks; described by its creator as "the open source CodeRabbit alternative," now free for personal/OSS use — offered as Dosu's migration path — [Pullfrog](https://pullfrog.com/), [GitHub: pullfrog/pullfrog](https://github.com/pullfrog/pullfrog)
- **GitHub's own answer**: "GitHub Agentic Workflows," described as a technical preview letting maintainers define triage logic in a Markdown file; the agent reads new issues, applies labels, posts acknowledgment comments, and detects duplicates — a first-party, still-experimental alternative to third-party bots — [via search synthesis, no single primary GitHub doc URL surfaced in this pass]
- **Cotera's published teardown** (case study, ~March 2026): built a custom Linear-API triage bot; it worked (daily triage time fell from 45 to 15 minutes) but the team calculated the total investment at 160 hours to save 65 hours and turned the bot off in month eight, concluding "that's not a good trade"; their eventual replacement triage agent explicitly "suggests but doesn't decide" — a rare, unusually candid published account of an AI-triage project being abandoned on cost/benefit grounds rather than a technical failure — [Cotera: Linear API automation guide](https://cotera.co/articles/linear-api-automation-guide)
- **Third-party assessment of Dosu's claimed results** (should be treated with caution, unverified by the reviewer): a third-party review claims Dosu resolves ~40% of issues autonomously without developer intervention — flagged explicitly here as an unverified reviewer claim, not an audited figure — [aiagentstore.ai: Dosu](https://aiagentstore.ai/ai-agent/dosu)

### Inferences
- Across Linear, Sentry, and Dosu, the consistent design choice is to keep the human decision at the *cheapest possible* gate — Linear surfaces a suggestion with a visible rationale rather than auto-merging; Sentry-Linear ties automatic resolution to a human explicitly marking the Linear issue "Done," not to any AI confidence score; Dosu's open-sourced tools default to suggest/label rather than auto-close except where a project explicitly configures otherwise. This is the same "gate on irreversibility, not confidence" principle found independently in the HITL governance literature (Key Q5) — it recurs across every real production system found, which strengthens confidence in it as a genuine, not merely theoretical, best practice.
- The Cotera case is an important counterweight to the vendor triage-bot marketing found throughout this research: even a triage bot that objectively works (45→15 minutes/day) can still be a bad investment once total build/maintenance effort is counted, and their post-mortem fix (a suggest-only agent instead of a decisive one) is itself evidence for the suggest-over-decide pattern rather than a one-off preference.
